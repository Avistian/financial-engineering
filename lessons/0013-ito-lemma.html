The Itô Integral & Itô's Lemma

Lesson 013 · The chain rule for random functions · ~60 min

Warm up

Last lesson ended on one strange fact: the squared wiggles of a Brownian path do not vanish, they pile up at rate one — (dW)² = dt. That looked like a curiosity. Today it becomes a tool. Almost everything you will price or hedge is some function of a random quantity: an option's value is a function of the stock price, the stock price is a function of a Brownian motion. To do anything with those functions you need a rule for "if the input wiggles a little, how does the output move?" In ordinary calculus that rule is the chain rule. For random inputs the ordinary chain rule is wrong, and the fix — one extra term — is Itô's lemma, the single most-used formula in all of derivatives pricing.

Here is the honest map. First we re-warm the ordinary chain rule as a two-term Taylor expansion, so you can see exactly which term ordinary calculus throws away. Then we meet the Itô integral — what "∫ … dW" even means — and the one design choice (evaluate at the left edge of each step) that makes it a fair game with no built-in drift. Then we keep the term ordinary calculus drops, substitute (dW)² = dt, and out falls Itô's lemma. We drive it home on two examples: d(W²), which re-derives last lesson's W² − t martingale, and d(log S) for a stock, which produces the famous −½σ² drift correction — the reason a volatile stock's typical path grows slower than its average.

The promise, made concrete. By the end you will be able to take any smooth function of a price, write down how it moves in one line, and say why it carries an extra drift term that a first-year calculus student would miss. You will have watched that extra term appear on screen as a visible upward drift, and watched it drag a volatile stock's median path below its mean. Nothing here needs machinery beyond the (dW)² = dt you proved in Lesson 012.

Re-warm: the ordinary chain rule is a two-term Taylor expansion

Before we can see what breaks, recall what works. Take a smooth function f — "smooth" meaning it has no kinks, so you can zoom in and it looks like a straight line. If its input x nudges by a tiny amount Δx, the output changes by an amount you can expand as a sum of ever-smaller pieces (this is the Taylor expansion — approximating a curve near a point by its slope, then its curvature, then finer corrections):

Δf = f′(x)·Δx + ½ f″(x)·(Δx)² + (even smaller terms)

In words: the first piece f′(x)·Δx is "slope times step" — how far a straight line of the right tilt would take you. Here f′ (read "f-prime") is the first derivative, the slope. The second piece uses f″ ("f-double-prime"), the second derivative, which measures curvature — how fast the slope itself is changing. It multiplies (Δx)², the square of the step.

Now the crucial move that ordinary calculus makes. For an ordinary smooth path, a step over a tiny time Δt has size Δx ≈ (speed)·Δt, so its square is (Δx)² ≈ (speed)²·(Δt)² — proportional to (Δt)², which is vanishingly small next to Δt itself. So the second term is thrown away, and only the first survives:

df = f′(x)·dx   — the ordinary chain rule

Takeaway: the ordinary chain rule keeps only "slope × step" because the squared step (Δx)² is negligible for a smooth path. That last assumption is exactly what Brownian motion breaks.

Re-warm: for Brownian motion the squared step is not negligible

Recall the headline of Lesson 012. A Brownian step over a tiny time Δt is ΔW ~ N(0, Δt) — a bell-curve draw with typical size √Δt. Square it and you get something of size Δt, and summed up these squares lock onto the elapsed time. We wrote that as the working rule

(dW)² = dt   (the squared Brownian step behaves like dt, not like 0)

So the exact term ordinary calculus discards — the one with (step)² in it — is, for Brownian motion, the same size as the terms it keeps. Refuse to discard it and you are forced into a new calculus. Two smaller cousins do still vanish and will be dropped throughout: dW·dt (size √Δt · Δt) and (dt)² both shrink faster than dt. The whole of Itô calculus is the bookkeeping rule below.

Product of small stepsIts sizeKeep or drop?
(dW)²Δtkeep → replace by dt
dW·dtΔt·√Δt = Δt1.5drop (→ 0)

What "∫ … dW" means: the Itô integral

Itô's lemma will be written with a dW in it, so first we must say what it means to "add up" against Brownian increments. Suppose you hold a position of size H in something whose random shock over a step is ΔW, and H can change over time. Your total random gain over [0, t] is the running sum of "position now × shock now." Chop the interval into small pieces t₀ < t₁ < … and add:

∫₀ᵗ Hs dWs  =  lim   Σi Hti-1 · (Wti − Wti-1)

Read it slowly. Wti − Wti-1 is the Brownian shock over one little step (the "coin flip"). Hti-1 is your position — and here is the one choice that defines the Itô integral: the position is taken at ti-1, the start (left edge) of the step, before the shock is revealed. That is not a technicality; it is the whole meaning. It says you must place your bet before the coin is flipped — you cannot use tomorrow's move to size today's position. In the language of Lesson 011, H is adapted: it depends only on information available now.

Why the left edge makes it a fair game (a martingale). Look at one term, Hti-1 · ΔWi. The position Hti-1 is known by time ti-1; the shock ΔWi is fresh, independent of the past, with mean 0 (Lesson 012's defining properties). So its expected contribution, given everything so far, is Hti-1 · E[ΔWi] = H·0 = 0 — exactly the "take out what is known, then the increment averages to zero" move you drilled in Lesson 011. Every term has conditional mean zero, so the whole Itô integral is a martingale: it has no drift, its best forecast is its current value. Hold on to this: anything of the form ∫ … dW contributes no average trend. All the drift in Itô's lemma will come from the other, non-random term.

Takeaway: ∫ H dW means "position (set at the start of each step) times the next shock, summed." Left-edge sizing ⇒ each term averages to zero ⇒ the integral is a martingale (zero drift).

Itô's lemma for a function of Brownian motion

Now put a smooth function on top of a Brownian motion: let Yt = f(Wt). How does Y move over a tiny step? Write the same Taylor expansion as before, but with input W instead of a smooth x:

df = f′(W)·dW + ½ f″(W)·(dW)² + (smaller terms)
Step A (keep the second-order term). In ordinary calculus we would drop (dW)². We cannot: for Brownian motion it is first-order.
Step B (substitute the working rule). Replace (dW)² by dt (Lesson 012). The "smaller terms" involve dW·dt and (dt)², which we drop.
Step C (read off the result). What remains is Itô's lemma for a function of Brownian motion:
df = f′(W)·dW + ½ f″(W)·dt

Two pieces, each with a plain meaning. f′(W)·dW is the random part (its proper name is the diffusion term): a position f′(W) riding the next Brownian shock — and, by the previous section, a martingale that averages to zero. ½ f″(W)·dt is the new term ordinary calculus misses: a steady, non-random drift, present only when the function is curved (f″ ≠ 0). Curvature plus wiggle manufactures drift out of nothing. That sentence is the whole lesson.

Why curvature makes drift, intuitively. If f curves upward (f″ > 0, like a valley), then a wiggle up helps you more than the equal wiggle down hurts you — the two do not cancel, and the leftover is a small gain each step. Averaged over the ceaseless wiggling, that leftover becomes a genuine upward drift of size ½ f″ per unit time. A straight line (f″ = 0) is symmetric — up and down cancel exactly — so it gets no correction, and Itô's lemma collapses back to the ordinary chain rule. (This is the continuous-time cousin of Jensen's inequality from Lesson 011: the average of a curved function sits above the function of the average.)

Worked example 1: d(W²), and last lesson's martingale falls out

Take the simplest curved function, f(x) = x². Its slope is f′(x) = 2x and its curvature is f″(x) = 2 (constant). Drop these into Itô's lemma with input W:

d(W²) = f′(W)·dW + ½ f″(W)·dt = 2W·dW + ½·2·dt = 2W·dW + dt

Compare the naive answer. A first-year student applies the ordinary chain rule and writes d(W²) = 2W·dW, full stop. They are missing the + dt — a steady drift of exactly 1 per unit time. Where does it come from? Nowhere but the squared wiggle: ½·f″·(dW)² = ½·2·dt = dt. Now "integrate" (add up the steps from 0 to t):

Wt² = ∫₀ᵗ 2Ws dWs + ∫₀ᵗ dt = (a martingale) + t

The first piece is an Itô integral, so it is a martingale with mean 0; the second piece is just t. Taking expectations, E[Wt²] = 0 + t = t, and rearranging, Wt² − t is a martingale. That is exactly the fact you proved by hand at the end of Lesson 012 — but now it is not a special trick, it is a one-line consequence of Itô's lemma. The leftover t that made W² a "fair game only after subtracting t" is the quadratic variation, showing up as the ½ f″ dt term.

You can watch this on screen. Below, we simulate many Brownian paths, square each one, and average. The ordinary chain rule says d(W²) = 2W dW is a pure martingale, so the average should stay flat at 0. Instead it climbs — and it climbs along the line y = t, which is exactly the accumulated ½ f″ dt = dt correction. Slide the control to average more paths and watch the empirical mean lock onto the drift line while the martingale part stays flat.

The correction term is a visible drift. For f(W) = W² we split each path exactly into Wt² = (∫2W dW) + Σ(ΔW)². The Itô integral ∫2W dW (what the naive chain rule keeps) averages to 0 — a flat martingale. The mean of Wt² climbs the dashed line y = t. The entire rise is the second piece, the (dW)² = dt term — i.e. ½ f″·t = t. Slide N (number of paths averaged) from 2 to 256: more paths, tighter lock onto the theory.

Takeaway: d(W²) = 2W dW + dt. The + dt is the Itô correction; it is why W² − t (not W²) is the martingale.

Predict before you read on

Here is where it starts to pay rent. A stock price S is usually modelled so that its percentage moves are Brownian: dS = μS·dt + σS·dW, where μ ("mu") is the average growth rate and σ ("sigma") is the volatility (the size of the random wiggle). Suppose you want the growth rate of the log price log S — the thing that actually adds up over time. A naive chain rule gives μ. What does Itô's lemma give?

The general Itô process, and the full lemma

To handle a stock we need a step up from bare Brownian motion. A real quantity has its own trend and its own wiggle size, so we write a general Itô process:

dX = a·dt + b·dW

In words: over a tiny step, X drifts by a·dt (a steady pull; a is the drift, the average trend per unit time) plus a random shock b·dW (b is the diffusion or volatility, how big the wiggle is). Both a and b may themselves depend on time and on X. Bare Brownian motion is the special case a = 0, b = 1.

First we need this process's squared step, because that is what feeds the correction. Square dX and apply the keep/drop table:

(dX)² = (a·dt + b·dW)² = a²(dt)² + 2ab·dt·dW + b²(dW)² = b²·dt

(The first two terms are dropped; only b²(dW)² = b²dt survives.) Now Taylor-expand a smooth function f(t, X) — of both time and the process — keeping first derivatives in each and the one second-order term in X that will not vanish. Here ft means "how f changes with time directly," fx "how it changes as X changes," and fxx its curvature in X:

Expand: df = ft·dt + fx·dX + ½ fxx·(dX)²  (the dt·dX and (dt)² pieces are negligible).
Substitute dX = a·dt + b·dW and (dX)² = b²·dt: df = ft·dt + fx(a·dt + b·dW) + ½ fxx·b²·dt.
Collect the dt terms and the dW term:
df = ( ft + a·fx + ½ b²·fxx ) dt  +  b·fx·dW

This is Itô's lemma in full. Read the two brackets. The dW part, b·fx·dW, is the random part (a martingale, zero average). The dt bracket is the drift of f, and it has three sources: ft (the function changing with time on its own), a·fx (the process's own drift carried through the slope), and the star of the show, ½ b²·fxx — the Itô correction, curvature times squared volatility. Set a = 0, b = 1, f(t,X) = f(W) and it collapses to the simpler df = f′ dW + ½ f″ dt from before.

Worked example 2: d(log S) and the −½σ² drag

Now the example that runs through the rest of Q2. Model the stock as geometric Brownian motion (GBM) — "geometric" because it is the percentage moves that are Brownian, so the price stays positive:

dS = μS·dt + σS·dW   (so the drift is a = μS, the diffusion is b = σS)

We want f(S) = log S, the log price — the quantity whose changes add up cleanly over time. Re-warm the calculus we need: the derivative of log S is 1/S, and the derivative of 1/S is −1/S². So ft = 0 (no direct time dependence), fx = 1/S, and fxx = −1/S². Feed these, with a = μS and b = σS, into the drift bracket and the dW term, in the slow lane:

Drift. ft + a·fx + ½ b²·fxx = 0 + μS·(1/S) + ½·(σS)²·(−1/S²). The stock price S cancels in each term: μS/S = μ and ½·σ²S²·(−1/S²) = −½σ². Drift = μ − ½σ².
Random part. b·fx = σS·(1/S) = σ. So the dW term is simply σ·dW.
d(log S) = ( μ − ½σ² ) dt + σ·dW

Look hard at that drift. The log price does not grow at μ; it grows at μ − ½σ². Volatility is subtracted. This is the volatility drag (also "variance drain"), and it is a direct consequence of the Itô correction — curvature of the log function (it bends downward, f″ < 0) turns the ceaseless wiggling into a steady penalty. Integrating gives the closed form you saw previewed in the Lesson 012 lab:

St = S₀ · exp( (μ − ½σ²)·t + σ·Wt )

Here is the practical bite, and it confuses professionals. The average (mean) price still grows at μ: E[St] = S₀·eμt. But the typical price — the median, the one in the middle of all outcomes — grows only at μ − ½σ². The mean is dragged up by rare huge winners (a lognormal has a long right tail); the path you are actually likely to live through grows slower. Crank up volatility and the gap widens. Slide the control below: at σ = 20% the median still rises, but at high volatility the median growth can go negative even while the mean keeps climbing at μ.

The volatility drag on a stock. Geometric Brownian motion with growth μ = 10%. The faint lines are sample price paths; the solid line is the mean E[St] = S₀eμt (it grows at μ, unaffected by σ); the dashed line is the median, growing at μ − ½σ². Slide σ from 5% to 80%: as volatility rises, the median falls away below the mean — most paths do worse than the average, and past σ ≈ 45% the median drift turns negative. That gap is ½σ², the Itô correction.

Takeaway: d(log S) = (μ − ½σ²)dt + σ dW. Average growth is μ; typical (median) growth is μ − ½σ². The ½σ² gap is pure Itô correction.

Explain it back

What this buys you

Three payoffs and one honest warning, then the checks.

  • Pricing PDEs come from here (Lesson 016). Apply Itô's lemma to an option's value V(t, S), then choose a hedge that cancels the random dW term. What is left is a pure dt equation with no randomness — the Black–Scholes PDE. The ½ b² fxx correction you just met is the second-derivative term in that PDE. Every derivative-pricing equation is Itô's lemma plus a hedging argument.
  • SDEs and simulation (Lesson 014). "dS = μS dt + σS dW" is a stochastic differential equation; its solution is the exp(…) above, found by applying Itô to log S. The same trick solves the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (mean-reverting) process next lesson. When you simulate GBM in code you use the solved form — with the −½σ² in it, or your prices will drift wrong.
  • It shows up in real P&L. The volatility drag is why a leveraged or volatile strategy can have a positive average return yet lose money for the typical investor; why rebalancing a portfolio is not free; and why "compound annual growth" is below "average annual return." The −½σ² is not exam trivia — it is a line item.
The warning (failure mode first, as always). Itô's lemma is exact for the model you feed it. Feed it geometric Brownian motion and you inherit all of Brownian motion's lies from Lesson 004: constant volatility (real vol clusters and jumps), Gaussian shocks (real tails are fat), and a single continuous W (real prices gap). The formula will faithfully propagate a false model into a false price. Later units fix the model — jumps (Lesson 018) add a term to Itô's lemma; stochastic volatility (Year 1 Q3) makes σ itself random. Learn Itô as the exact machinery; never confuse "exact given the model" with "true."
Trap — the six ways this material is misused. (1) Using the ordinary chain rule on a random input. Writing d(W²) = 2W·dW drops the + dt. Every function of a random process needs the ½ f″ dt term unless f″ = 0. (2) Forgetting the drift of log S is μ − ½σ², not μ. This single sign-and-half error corrupts option prices, Monte-Carlo simulators, and Sharpe estimates. (3) Keeping dW·dt or (dt)². Only (dW)² = dt survives; the other products are higher order and vanish. (4) Evaluating the integrand at the right edge. The Itô integral takes the position at the start of each step. Use the end (or midpoint) and you get a different calculus (Stratonovich) with no martingale property — and, in finance, a look-ahead that does not exist. (5) Thinking the dW term carries drift. It never does: ∫ … dW is always a martingale. All average trend lives in the dt bracket. (6) Assuming mean = median for a stock. For lognormal prices the mean grows at μ but the typical path grows at μ − ½σ²; quoting the mean as "what you'll get" overstates the outcome.

Check your understanding

Common questions (and honest answers)

“Why do we keep (dW)² but drop (dt)² and dW·dt — isn't that inconsistent?”
It only looks inconsistent until you weigh them. Over a step of length Δt, a Brownian move is about √Δt, so (dW)² is about Δt — the same size as the terms we keep, so it must stay. But (dt)² is Δt² and dW·dt is about Δt·√Δt = Δt1.5 — both shrink strictly faster than Δt, so when you sum roughly 1/Δt of them the totals still go to zero. Consistency is precisely about size: keep anything of order Δt, drop anything smaller.
“Isn't Itô's lemma just Taylor's theorem? Why the fancy name?”
It is Taylor's theorem — with one substitution the ordinary version never makes. Ordinary calculus stops at first order because the squared step is negligible; Itô keeps the second-order term and replaces (dW)² by dt. The "lemma" packages that single rule so you do not re-derive it each time. The surprising part is not the algebra — it is that a term everyone else throws away creates a real, tradeable drift.
“Why the left endpoint? Wouldn't the midpoint be more accurate?”
The midpoint (the Stratonovich integral) is used in physics, and it obeys the ordinary chain rule with no correction term. But it quietly peeks at the future: the midpoint value depends on the move you are about to make. In finance that is forbidden — you size a position before the price moves, not after. The left-endpoint (Itô) choice is the one where "position now × next shock" has mean zero, which is why Itô integrals are martingales and why the whole risk-neutral pricing story (Lesson 015) works. The correction term is the price you pay for honesty about time.
“Do quants actually apply Itô's lemma by hand on the job?”
Deriving a new model's dynamics, yes — on a whiteboard, exactly the two moves in this lesson. Day-to-day you more often use its consequences: the −½σ² in every GBM simulator, the second-derivative (“gamma”) term in a hedging P&L, the Black–Scholes PDE. In interviews for any derivatives-adjacent seat, "apply Itô to log S" and "why does W² need the −t" are standard. The mechanics are the asset; the intuition (curvature × wiggle = drift) is what separates a memoriser from someone who can price a new payoff.
“If the mean price grows at μ, how can the typical path do worse? Where does the money go?”
Nowhere — it is concentrated. Lognormal outcomes are lopsided: most paths land a bit below the starting trend, while a few land enormously above. The average is propped up entirely by those rare giant winners, so it exceeds the median. No money is lost from the average; it is just that "average" and "what you'll most likely experience" are different questions, and the gap between them is ½σ² per unit time. Higher volatility, wider gap.

Vocabulary you now own

  • Itô integral ∫H dW — the limit of "position (set at the start of each step) × next Brownian shock," summed. Left-endpoint sizing makes every term average to zero, so the integral is a martingale (no drift).
  • Itô's lemma (for f(W)) — df = f′(W)·dW + ½ f″(W)·dt; the chain rule for a function of Brownian motion, with an extra curvature-driven drift term.
  • Itô process — dX = a·dt + b·dW: a drift a plus a random part of size b. Its squared step is (dX)² = b²·dt.
  • Itô's lemma (general) — df = (ft + a·fx + ½ b²·fxx) dt + b·fx·dW. The dW term is a martingale; all drift is in the dt bracket.
  • Itô correction — the ½ b² fxx (or ½ f″) drift term; its sign is the sign of the curvature. Zero for linear functions.
  • Geometric Brownian motion (GBM) — dS = μS·dt + σS·dW, solved to St = S₀·exp((μ − ½σ²)t + σWt); the standard positive-price model.
  • Volatility drag (variance drain) — the −½σ² gap between a stock's average growth μ and its typical (median) growth μ − ½σ².
  • Drift / diffusion — the dt part (average trend) and the dW part (random wiggle) of a process.
