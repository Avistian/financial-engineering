Warm up

Quarter 1 was about not fooling yourself with data you already have. Quarter 2 is a different animal: it builds the mathematical language in which prices, hedges, and option values are actually written. Over the next ten units you will construct Brownian motion, learn Itô's lemma, solve stochastic differential equations, change probability measures, and price a European option three independent ways. All of it — every single unit — rests on two ideas introduced today: what an observer knows at time t, and the best guess of an unknown quantity given what is known. Those are the filtration and the conditional expectation.

Here is the honest warning and the honest reassurance. The warning: this is the start of the most mathematically demanding stretch of the whole three-year track, and the notation is unfamiliar — curly ℱ's, subscripted σ-algebras, phrases like "almost surely." The reassurance: today's mathematics is arithmetic on a tree with eight outcomes. Every abstraction below is introduced only after you have seen the concrete thing it names, and every formula is checked against numbers you can verify by hand. There is no step in this lesson you cannot do with a pencil.

One more promise, to keep the effort honest: by the end you will have computed the price of a call option (7.475, on the tree below) using nothing but conditional expectation. Not a formula you were handed — a number you built.

Why Q1's probability is not enough

You already know probability: random variables, expectations, variance, the CLT (Lesson 005). So why relearn the foundations with heavier machinery? Because three specific things you are about to do break the undergraduate setup, and each break is repaired by exactly one piece of today's vocabulary.

Break 1 — "what did the trader know?" has no expression. A backtest is a sequence of decisions, each made with only the information available at that instant. In undergraduate probability there is no object that is "the information available at time t." You have random variables and you have their distributions, but nothing that says this rule may only look at the past. That missing object is a σ-algebra, and the growing family of them is a filtration. This is not decoration: leakage — the sin the whole of Q1 was built to detect — becomes a precise, checkable statement about which σ-algebra a quantity is measurable with respect to.
Break 2 — in continuous time, every individual outcome has probability zero. Prices move in continuous time, so a single "outcome" is an entire path of the price over a whole year. There are uncountably many such paths, and each specific one has probability exactly 0 — so a definition of the form "add up the probabilities of the outcomes where the option pays off" adds up zeros and returns nothing. The repair is to assign probability to sets of outcomes (events) from the start, which is what a measure does, and to stop expecting outcomes to carry mass individually.
Break 3 — pricing changes the probabilities. The central move of derivatives pricing (unit 015) is to compute an expectation under a different probability measure than the real one — the "risk-neutral" measure. To even state that sentence you need probability to be an object you can swap: a measure P on a fixed space of outcomes, replaceable by another measure Q on the same space. Undergraduate probability blurs the space, the measure, and the distribution together, so it cannot express the swap. Measure theory separates them, and that separation is the whole point.

Takeaway: measure theory is not rigour for its own sake here. It is the minimum vocabulary needed to say "what was known when," "how do we assign probability when single outcomes are impossible," and "let us now change the probabilities."

What we will not do: construct the Lebesgue integral, prove the monotone convergence theorem, or worry about non-measurable sets. Those are Shreve's Chapter 1 appendices and they buy you nothing at the bench. This is measure theory lite: the four objects (Ω, ℱ, P, and conditional expectation), their working rules, and total fluency on a finite tree.

Ω: the set of possible futures

Start with the concrete object we will use for the entire lesson. A stock begins at S₀ = 100. Over each of three periods it either goes up (multiplied by u = 1.1) or down (multiplied by d = 0.9). Write H for an up move and T for a down move, so a complete history is a string of three letters like HTH.

The sample space Ω (capital omega) is the set of all complete histories — all the ways the world could turn out, at the finest resolution the model admits. Here there are 2³ = 8 of them:

Ω = { HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT }

Two things to internalise about Ω, because they are what make it different from "the set of prices." First, an element ω ∈ Ω (a single lowercase omega) is an entire future, not a state at one moment: it fixes what happens at every date. Second, Ω is fixed once and for all before any probabilities are mentioned. It is the stage; the probability measure is the lighting.

Now the numbers. The price at time t depends only on the first t letters of ω, and the quantity we will condition on all lesson is the payoff of a European call option struck at K = 100 — a contract that pays whatever S₃ exceeds 100, and nothing if S₃ ends at or below 100:

V(ω) = max(S₃(ω) − 100, 0)   — written (S₃ − K)⁺

Every outcome, its price path, and its payoff (verify a row or two by hand — 100 × 1.1 × 0.9 × 1.1 = 108.9):

A σ-algebra is a list of questions you can answer

Here is the intuition, before any symbol. Imagine you are standing at time 1. One flip has happened; you saw it. Somebody asks you yes/no questions about the future:

  • "Did the first move go up?" — you can answer that. You watched it.
  • "Will the option finish in the money?" — you cannot answer that yet. It depends on flips you have not seen.

So your position at time 1 is completely described by the list of questions you are able to answer. Each yes/no question about the world corresponds to the set of outcomes for which the answer is "yes" — for instance the question "did the first move go up?" is the set {HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT}, which we abbreviate {H··}. A set of outcomes is called an event. Therefore:

a σ-algebra ℱ = the collection of events whose "did it happen?" you can settle

That is all a σ-algebra is — a collection of subsets of Ω, thought of as resolvable questions. The name is intimidating; the object is a to-do list of answerable questions. (It is also called a σ-field; the two words mean the same thing, and Shreve uses both.)

Why the axioms are obvious once you read them this way

Textbooks define a σ-algebra ℱ on Ω by three requirements. Read each as a statement about answering questions and it stops being arbitrary:

(i) Ω ∈ ℱ. The question "did something happen?" is answerable — yes, trivially. (And so is its opposite, "did nothing happen?", giving ∅ ∈ ℱ.)
(ii) If A ∈ ℱ then Aᶜ ∈ ℱ (the complement, "not A"). If you can settle whether the first move went up, you can settle whether it did not go up. Knowing an answer means knowing its negation.
(iii) If A₁, A₂, A₃, … ∈ ℱ then A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ … ∈ ℱ (countably many unions). If you can settle each of a list of questions, you can settle "did at least one of them happen?" Combined with (ii) you also get intersections, because "both A and B" is "not (not-A or not-B)" — the same trick as De Morgan's law.

Why countably many and not arbitrarily many? Because with arbitrary unions the requirement becomes so strong that consistent probability assignment collapses: in a continuous setting you could union together uncountably many single points, each of probability zero, and demand that the total be zero — which would force intervals to have zero length. Countable additivity is the exact amount of combining that keeps measure well behaved. You will never test this at the bench; you just need to know that "σ" in σ-algebra means "closed under countable operations," and that this is why.

The three σ-algebras of our tree, written out

Now build them explicitly for the price model. At time 0, before any flip, the only questions you can settle are the trivial ones:

ℱ₀ = { ∅, Ω }   (2 events — "you know nothing")

At time 1, you have seen the first flip, so you can additionally settle "was it up?" and "was it down?":

ℱ₁ = { ∅, Ω, {H··}, {T··} }   (4 events)

At time 2 you can settle any question about the first two flips: the four basic events {HH·}, {HT·}, {TH·}, {TT·}, plus every union of them — for example "did the two flips differ?" is {HT·} ∪ {TH·}. Counting all unions of 4 basic blocks gives 2⁴ = 16 events. At time 3 everything is known, so every subset of Ω is answerable: 2⁸ = 256 events. Written as a sequence, the number of answerable questions goes 2, 4, 16, 256 — information exploding as resolution improves.

Takeaway: σ-algebra = answerable questions. Bigger σ-algebra = more information.

Information as a partition: the atoms of ℱt

There is a second, more visual way to hold the same idea, and it is the one you should picture when you read "conditional expectation" later. Instead of listing answerable questions, ask: which outcomes can I still not tell apart?

At time 1, having seen H, you cannot distinguish HHH from HHT from HTH from HTT — as far as your information goes, you are somewhere in that block of four and cannot say where. Your information therefore chops Ω into cells (formally: atoms) — groups of outcomes that are indistinguishable to you. A finite σ-algebra and a partition of Ω into cells are two descriptions of the same thing: the answerable events are exactly the unions of cells.

Time 0 is one cell of 8 (total blur). Time 1 is two cells of 4. Time 2 is four cells of 2. Time 3 is eight cells of 1 — perfect resolution. Slide the control below and watch the blur resolve.

The tree of paths (not prices — HT and TH stay separate branches even though both reach 99). Solid dark edges are flips already observed at time t; faint edges are still in the future. Each shaded band is one atom of ℱt: a set of outcomes your time-t information cannot tell apart. At t = 0 a single band covers all 8 outcomes; each step halves the bands until, at t = 3, every outcome sits alone. The readout also counts the answerable questions, 2 raised to the number of atoms.

The filtration: information that only grows

Line those σ-algebras up in time and you have the central object of continuous-time finance. A filtration is a family {ℱ_t}, one σ-algebra per date, that is increasing:

ℱ₀ ⊆ ℱ₁ ⊆ ℱ₂ ⊆ ℱ₃   (information accumulates; nothing is ever forgotten)

The inclusion is the whole content: anything you could answer yesterday you can still answer today. Modelling a market means fixing a filtration — the record of what an observer has seen — and then insisting that every object in the model respect it.

That insistence has a name. A process {X_t} is adapted to the filtration if, for every t, the value X_t is determined by time-t information — in the language of the previous section, X_t is constant on each cell of ℱt. We say X_t is ℱt-measurable, and it is worth translating that phrase once and for all:

"X is ℱt-measurable"  =  "X's value is knowable at time t"
The link back to Q1 — leakage is a measurability violation. A trading strategy is a process Δ_t: how many shares you hold at time t. Requiring Δ_t to be ℱt-measurable is exactly the no-look-ahead rule you spent Q1 enforcing by hand. Consider the leak from Lesson 009: standardising a signal with the full sample's mean and volatility. In today's language, that standardised signal is ℱT-measurable (it depends on the end of the sample) while being used as if it were ℱt-measurable. It is not a modelling slip; it is a type error. From now on you have a one-line test for the most expensive bug in quantitative research: is every quantity in my decision rule measurable with respect to the information I actually had?

Takeaway: filtration = the growing record of what is known. Adapted = never peeks ahead.

P: why probability lives on sets, not on outcomes

The third object is the probability measure P. It is a function that eats an event (a member of ℱ) and returns a number in [0, 1], subject to two requirements: P(Ω) = 1, and countable additivity — if A₁, A₂, … are disjoint events then P(A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ …) = P(A₁) + P(A₂) + …. The triple (Ω, ℱ, P) is called a probability space, and it is the complete specification of a probabilistic model: the outcomes, the answerable questions, the weights.

On our tree, take each flip to be up with probability p = ½, independently. Then every one of the 8 paths has probability (½)³ = ⅛, and the probability of any event is (number of paths in it)/8. For example P({H··}) = 4/8 = ½ and P(V > 0) = P({HHH, HHT, HTH, THH}) = 4/8 = ½: the call finishes in the money exactly half the time.

The part that motivates the whole framework

Why insist that P eats sets rather than outcomes? On a finite tree the distinction is invisible — you can hand out ⅛ per path and rebuild everything. It becomes unavoidable the moment the space is continuous, so let us take the smallest example. Let Ω = [0, 1] with the uniform distribution. What is the probability of drawing exactly 0.5? It is 0: if it were any positive number ε, then since there are infinitely many points you could find 1/ε + 1 of them and their total probability would exceed 1. Yet P([0.4, 0.6]) = 0.2, comfortably positive. So every single outcome has probability zero, while sets of them have positive probability. Any theory that starts from "the probability of each outcome" is dead on arrival here; a theory that starts from sets is fine.

This is precisely the situation in unit 012, where an outcome is a whole Brownian path. Each individual path has probability zero. Only sets of paths ("the price stays above 90 all year") carry mass. Today's tree is the finite rehearsal for that.

One vocabulary item follows immediately. An event of probability zero is not impossible, merely negligible; so mathematicians say a statement holds almost surely (a.s.) when it holds except on a set of probability zero. When Lesson 013 says "the quadratic variation of Brownian motion equals t almost surely," it means: there are freak paths where it fails, they have total probability 0, and no expectation, price, or hedge is affected by them. "Almost surely" is the polite way of saying "we are allowed to ignore the pathological cases."

Takeaway: P assigns mass to events, not outcomes — which is the only thing that survives the jump to continuous time. "Almost surely" = "except on a probability-zero set."

Random variables: numbers that may or may not be knowable yet

A random variable is a function from Ω to the real numbers: it reads an outcome and returns a number. You have used this idea informally since Lesson 005; the only addition now is that we care which σ-algebra a random variable is measurable with respect to, because that is what says when its value becomes known.

Check the three cases on our tree, from the table above:

  • S₁ is ℱ₁-measurable: it equals 110 on the whole cell {H··} and 90 on the whole cell {T··} — constant on every cell of ℱ₁, so time-1 information pins it down.
  • S₂ is not ℱ₁-measurable: inside the cell {H··} it takes the value 121 (on HH·) and 99 (on HT·). At time 1 you cannot yet say which, so S₂ is not knowable at time 1. It is ℱ₂-measurable.
  • V = (S₃ − 100)⁺ is ℱ₃-measurable and nothing smaller: it varies inside every cell of ℱ₂ (e.g. cell {HH·} holds payoffs 33.1 and 8.9). The option's payoff is not known until the end. That is the entire reason we need conditional expectation.

A subtlety worth ten minutes: knowing the price is not knowing the path

Return to the coincidence we flagged: S₂ = 99 on both HT· and TH·. Suppose your information were only the current price, not the sequence of moves. The events you could resolve would be generated by the values of S₂, a σ-algebra written σ(S₂). Its cells are {HH·}, {HT·, TH·} (fused!), and {TT·} — three cells, not four. So σ(S₂) ⊂ ℱ₂, strictly coarser: a price watcher genuinely knows less than a tape watcher, and cannot answer "did the market go up first?"

This is not a curiosity. It is the mathematical statement of why microstructure exists as a field: order flow carries information that the price level alone has thrown away (Year 2 Q3, units 061–067 — order-flow imbalance is a signal precisely because σ(price) ⊊ σ(order flow)). And it explains why "which σ-algebra?" is always a real question with real money attached, not bookkeeping.

Conditional expectation: your best guess, given what you know

Now the load-bearing concept of the entire quarter. Time 1 has arrived, the first move was up, and somebody asks what the option is worth. You cannot say 33.1 or 0 — the last two flips are unwritten. But you are not helpless either: you know you are in the top half of the tree, where payoffs are larger. The honest answer is an average over what remains uncertain, using only what you know. That is conditional expectation.

Before the machinery, commit to a prediction about what kind of object the answer is. This is the single most common confusion in the subject, so it is worth being wrong here in private:

Layer 0 — recall plain expectation

From Lesson 005: for a random variable X on a finite space, E[X] = Σ_ω X(ω)·P(ω) — value times probability, summed over outcomes. For our call, with each path carrying ⅛:

E[V] = (33.1 + 8.9 + 8.9 + 0 + 8.9 + 0 + 0 + 0)/8 = 59.8/8 = 7.475

One number. It is the best guess before anything has happened — the view from time 0.

Layer 1 — conditioning on a single event

Suppose you learn only that the event A = {H··} occurred (the first move was up). The recipe you already know: restrict attention to the outcomes in A, and renormalise so that the probabilities inside A sum to 1.

E[V | A] = E[V·1A] / P(A)

Here 1_A is the indicator of A — the random variable equal to 1 on A and 0 off it — so V·1_A is "V where A happened, zero elsewhere." Numerically, the four outcomes in A have payoffs 33.1, 8.9, 8.9, 0, so E[V·1_A] = 50.9/8 = 6.3625, and P(A) = ½:

E[V | {H··}] = 6.3625 / 0.5 = 12.725

Same computation for the other branch: payoffs 8.9, 0, 0, 0 give E[V | {T··}] = (8.9/8)/0.5 = 2.225. Both are single numbers, each attached to one event.

Layer 2 — conditioning on a σ-algebra (the real definition)

You will rarely condition on one event. You condition on your whole information set, which means doing Layer 1 in every cell at once and gluing the answers together into a single object:

E[V | ℱ₁](ω) = 12.725 if ω ∈ {H··},   2.225 if ω ∈ {T··}

Look carefully at what that is. It assigns a number to every outcome ω, so it is a random variable, not a number — the answer to the prediction above. Two properties characterise it, and both are visible in the display:

  • It is ℱ₁-measurable: constant on each cell of ℱ₁. It must be — it is an answer you are able to state at time 1, so it cannot depend on flips you have not seen.
  • It preserves averages cell by cell: on {H··} it holds the value 12.725, which is precisely the average of V over that cell. It coarsens V without moving any local mean.

Why does it have to be a random variable? Because at the moment you write down the formula you do not know which cell you will land in. "Given what I will know at time 1, my estimate is 12.725 if the first move is up and 2.225 if it is down" — that is one object, defined on all of Ω, whose value is revealed when time 1 arrives. Conditional expectation is a plan for guessing, not a guess.

Repeat the exercise at time 2, where there are four cells of two outcomes each: E[V|ℱ₂] = 21.00 on {HH·} (average of 33.1 and 8.9), 4.45 on {HT·} and also on {TH·}, and 0 on {TT·}. The finer your information, the more values the conditional expectation can take, and the closer it hugs V. In the limit E[V|ℱ₃] = V: with full information, guessing stops and you simply read off the answer.

Slide through the whole family below. Read it as backward averaging: each node is the average of its two children, so moving left blurs the payoff step by step, and the numbers on the highlighted column are the values of E[V|ℱ_t].

The same path tree, now labelled with E[V | ℱlevel] for the call payoff V = (S₃ − 100)⁺. The right-hand column (t = 3) is V itself: 33.1, 8.9, 8.9, 0, 8.9, 0, 0, 0. Slide t and the highlighted column is the random variable E[V|ℱ_t] — four values at t = 2 (21.00, 4.45, 4.45, 0.00), two at t = 1 (12.725, 2.225), and one at t = 0 (7.475, the unconditional mean). Every node equals the average of its two children, which is why re-averaging one column reproduces the column to its left.

You just priced an option. That 7.475 at the root is not a toy number. With zero interest rates, the arbitrage-free price of this call is E[V] computed under the probability that makes the stock a fair game — and with u = 1.1, d = 0.9 that probability is exactly p* = (1 − d)/(u − d) = 0.1/0.2 = ½, the one we used. So the call is worth 7.475 today. Why that particular probability and not the real-world one is the content of unit 015 (Girsanov, risk-neutral pricing) — for now just register that pricing will turn out to be a conditional expectation under a carefully chosen measure, and you already know how to compute those.

The general definition — and why it looks so strange

Layer 2's recipe ("average within each cell") is perfect on a tree and useless in continuous time, where the cells are single paths of probability zero and dividing by P(cell) divides by zero. So the general definition is stated in a way that never divides. Y = E[X | ℱ] is defined as the random variable satisfying:

(1) Y is ℱ-measurable   and   (2) E[Y·1A] = E[X·1A] for every A ∈ ℱ

Condition (1) is "the answer uses only what you know." Condition (2), called partial averaging, is "on every question you can ask, Y and X have the same total mass." Notice what has been avoided: no division, no probabilities of individual outcomes, so it survives the continuous case unchanged. (One can prove such a Y always exists and is unique up to probability-zero sets — the existence proof is Shreve's appendix and you may take it on trust.)

Let us verify (2) by hand on the tree so the abstract condition becomes a number, taking A = {H··} ∈ ℱ₁:

Left side. Y = E[V|ℱ₁] equals 12.725 throughout A, so E[Y·1_A] = 12.725 × P(A) = 12.725 × 0.5 = 6.3625.
Right side. E[V·1_A] sums V over the four outcomes of A, each of probability ⅛: (33.1 + 8.9 + 8.9 + 0)/8 = 50.9/8 = 6.3625.
They match exactly — and they match for every A in ℱ₁ (there are four: ∅, {H··}, {T··}, Ω; check {T··}: 2.225 × 0.5 = 1.1125 versus 8.9/8 = 1.1125). So our cell-averaging answer is the conditional expectation in the general sense, not merely something that resembles it.

Takeaway: same object, two descriptions. On a tree: average within each cell. In general: the ℱ-measurable variable that reproduces X's mass on every event of ℱ.

Three rules you will use for the next two years

Almost every manipulation in Q2 is one of the following three moves. Learn them as reflexes, with the tree numbers as your proof that they are not magic.

Rule 1 — the tower property (iterated conditioning)

E[ E[X | ℱ_t] | ℱ_s ] = E[X | ℱ_s]   for s ≤ t   ("the coarser one wins")

In words: a guess about a guess collapses to the coarser guess. If today you forecast what you will think tomorrow, your forecast must equal what you think today — otherwise you would already have changed your mind. Check it: E[V|ℱ₂] takes values 21.00, 4.45, 4.45, 0. Average the first two (the two ℱ₂-cells sitting inside {H··}, equally likely): (21.00 + 4.45)/2 = 12.725 — exactly E[V|ℱ₁] on {H··}. And the other branch: (4.45 + 0)/2 = 2.225. ✓ Take s = 0 and the tower gives the most used special case of all:

E[ E[X | ℱ_t] ] = E[X]   — here (12.725 + 2.225)/2 = 7.475 ✓

That is how you will compute nearly every price: condition on something convenient, then average out.

Rule 2 — taking out what is known

E[ Y·X | ℱ ] = Y · E[ X | ℱ ]   when Y is ℱ-measurable

If a factor is already known, it is a constant as far as this conditioning is concerned, so it comes out of the expectation. Example on the tree: S₁ is ℱ₁-measurable, so E[S₁·V | ℱ₁] = S₁·E[V|ℱ₁], which equals 110 × 12.725 = 1399.75 on the up branch and 90 × 2.225 = 200.25 on the down branch. Verify the up branch the long way: average S₁·V over the cell {H··} where S₁ ≡ 110, giving 110 × (33.1 + 8.9 + 8.9 + 0)/4 = 110 × 12.725. ✓ This rule is what makes hedging algebra tractable: your position Δ_t is known at t, so it slides out of every time-t conditional expectation.

Rule 3 — independence kills conditioning

E[ X | ℱ ] = E[ X ]   when X is independent of ℱ

Information about things unrelated to X does not change your best guess. On the tree, the third flip is independent of ℱ₂, so E[third flip is H | ℱ₂] = ½ everywhere. This is the rule that makes Brownian increments so pleasant later: a future increment is independent of the past, so conditioning on the past leaves only its (zero) mean.

Two more properties, stated for completeness because you will see them used: conditional expectation is linear (E[aX + bZ|ℱ] = aE[X|ℱ] + bE[Z|ℱ]), and it satisfies conditional Jensen: for a convex function φ, E[φ(X)|ℱ] ≥ φ(E[X|ℱ]). Jensen's intuition, in one sentence you can reconstruct: averaging inside a convex function loses the value of dispersion, so averaging first and then applying φ gives less. Concretely on our tree with the convex payoff φ(x) = (x − 100)⁺: on the cell {HH·}, E[φ(S₃)|ℱ₂] = 21.00 while φ(E[S₃|ℱ₂]) = φ(121) = 21.00 — equal here because both branch values are above the strike so φ is linear there; but on {HT·}, E[φ(S₃)|ℱ₂] = 4.45 versus φ(99) = 0, a strict gap of 4.45. That gap is option value from uncertainty, the first glimpse of why volatility is worth money (units 016–017).

Conditional expectation is a projection

Everything above is computational. This section is the idea that makes conditional expectation feel inevitable, and it connects Q2 straight back to the linear algebra of Lesson 008 and the regression of Lesson 009.

Step 1 — random variables as vectors (re-warming Lesson 008)

Recall from Lesson 008 that a vector space needs only addition and scaling, and that a dot product gives it geometry: lengths, angles, and the notion of two vectors being perpendicular (dot product zero). Random variables on our tree add and scale like vectors — indeed a random variable on 8 outcomes is a list of 8 numbers. Define the inner product and the length:

⟨X, Z⟩ = E[X·Z]   and   ‖X‖ = √E[X²]

Sanity check that this is the same dot product you know: with each outcome carrying probability ⅛, E[X·Z] = Σ_ω X(ω)Z(ω)/8 — the ordinary dot product of two 8-vectors, rescaled. Nothing new has been invented; probability just supplies the weights. The space of random variables with finite E[X²], equipped with this inner product, is written L², and "orthogonal" (E[XZ] = 0) means, for mean-zero variables, exactly uncorrelated.

Step 2 — your information is a subspace

Which vectors in this space are guesses you are allowed to make at time 1? The ℱ₁-measurable ones: constant on {H··}, constant on {T··}. Such a variable is pinned down by two numbers (the value on each cell), so the allowed guesses form a 2-dimensional subspace of the 8-dimensional space. Information, geometrically, is a subspace — and a finer filtration is a bigger subspace (ℱ₂-measurable variables form a 4-dimensional one, and ℱ₃ gives all 8 dimensions).

Step 3 — the claim, and the slow-lane proof

Claim. Among all guesses your information allows, E[X|ℱ] is the one closest to X in the mean-squared sense:

E[X|ℱ] = argminY is ℱ-measurable E[(X − Y)²]

Here is the derivation with every step and its licence. Write Ŷ = E[X|ℱ] and let Y be any ℱ-measurable candidate.

Step A (algebra only). Add and subtract Ŷ: X − Y = (X − Ŷ) + (Ŷ − Y). Nothing has been assumed; we split the error into "the part even the best guess cannot remove" plus "how far Y sits from the best guess."
Step B (expand the square). (X − Y)² = (X − Ŷ)² + 2(X − Ŷ)(Ŷ − Y) + (Ŷ − Y)². This is just (a+b)² = a² + 2ab + b².
Step C (take expectations). By linearity of expectation, E[(X−Y)²] = E[(X−Ŷ)²] + 2E[(X−Ŷ)(Ŷ−Y)] + E[(Ŷ−Y)²].
Step D (the cross term dies — the only real work). Let Z = Ŷ − Y. Z is ℱ-measurable, being the difference of two ℱ-measurable variables. Now:
E[(X−Ŷ)·Z] = E[ E[(X−Ŷ)·Z | ℱ] ]  — licensed by the tower property with s = 0 (Rule 1);
  = E[ Z · E[X−Ŷ | ℱ] ]  — licensed by taking out what is known (Rule 2), legal precisely because Z is ℱ-measurable;
  = E[ Z · (E[X|ℱ] − Ŷ) ]  — licensed by linearity of conditional expectation, plus the fact that Ŷ is ℱ-measurable so E[Ŷ|ℱ] = Ŷ;
  = E[ Z · 0 ] = 0  — because Ŷ is E[X|ℱ], by definition.
Step E (conclude). The cross term is zero, so E[(X−Y)²] = E[(X−Ŷ)²] + E[(Ŷ−Y)²]. The second term is a square, hence ≥ 0, and equals 0 only when Y = Ŷ (almost surely). Therefore no allowed guess beats E[X|ℱ], and only E[X|ℱ] attains the minimum. ∎

Step E is Pythagoras: total squared error = unavoidable error + squared distance from the projection. And Step D is the geometric heart — the residual X − E[X|ℱ] is orthogonal to every ℱ-measurable variable. If you have seen "residuals ⟂ regressors" in Lesson 009, this is the same statement with "regressors" upgraded to "everything you know."

The widget makes the geometry and the arithmetic agree exactly. Using our tree with X = V and ℱ = ℱ₁, the minimum mean squared error is E[(V − E[V|ℱ₁])²] = 83.22, so V sits a perpendicular distance √83.22 = 9.12 above the plane of allowed guesses. Step off the projection by s units and Pythagoras says you pay exactly s² more:

A 2-D slice of the 8-dimensional space of random variables. The horizontal line is the subspace of guesses your time-1 information allows, parameterised as Y(s) = E[V|ℱ₁] + s·Z for a fixed ℱ₁-measurable direction Z with E[Z²] = 1. The dot above the line is the call payoff V, which no time-1 guess can reach: its perpendicular distance 9.12 is the irreducible uncertainty. The squared error reads 83.22 + s² and bottoms out at s = 0, where the error vector meets the subspace at 90° — the picture and the algebra of Step E are the same fact.

The bonus: variance decomposes

Pythagoras with Y = E[X] (the constant guess, which is certainly measurable) gives a decomposition you will meet again in every risk model:

Var(X) = E[ Var(X | ℱ) ] + Var( E[X | ℱ] )

Read it as: total uncertainty = what your information cannot explain + what it can. On our tree Var(V) = 110.78, the residual part is 83.22, and the explained part is Var(E[V|ℱ₁]) = 27.56; indeed 83.22 + 27.56 = 110.78. ✓ The ratio 27.56/110.78 = 24.9% is the fraction of the payoff's variance that the first flip explains — and if that phrasing sounds like R², it should: R² is this decomposition restricted to linear guesses.

What this buys you: alpha is a conditional expectation

Take a breath here, because the projection view silently reframes everything you did in Q1 and everything you will do in Year 2.

A signal is a bet that tomorrow's return is predictable from what you know today. In this language, the object you are trying to estimate is

E[ rt+1 | ℱt ]   — the best possible forecast from today's information

and an edge exists exactly when this conditional expectation is not constant. The efficient-market claim, stated precisely, is that this conditional expectation is (near) zero for tradable information sets. Alpha is not "a number a model outputs"; alpha is the assertion that a particular conditional expectation is non-zero and estimable after costs.

Three consequences that should reorganise how you read your own Q1 work:

  • Regression was a restricted projection. Lesson 009's OLS found the best guess of r_{t+1} among linear functions of one signal. Conditional expectation is the best guess among all functions of everything you know. So OLS is the projection onto a small subspace of the one that matters — which is exactly why non-linear models (trees, nets: Year 1 Q4, Year 2) can add value, and equally why they overfit: the bigger subspace fits noise as happily as signal.
  • The residual is the unpredictable part, by construction. Step D said r_{t+1} − E[r_{t+1}|ℱ_t] is orthogonal to everything in ℱ_t. So after you have extracted the conditional expectation, no further function of today's information can help. That is what "no remaining edge" means mathematically.
  • Leakage is conditioning on the wrong σ-algebra. Every leak in Q1 amounts to computing E[r_{t+1} | 𝒢] for some 𝒢 strictly bigger than ℱ_t and reporting it as if it were E[r_{t+1} | ℱ_t]. Because a bigger subspace always fits better (Step E: more dimensions, smaller residual), the leak always flatters the backtest. Purged cross-validation (Year 2 Q2) is nothing but machinery for keeping 𝒢 inside ℱ_t.

Explain it back

Martingales: the fair game

One definition remains, and it is the one that turns all this into pricing. An adapted process {M_t} (with E|M_t| < ∞) is a martingale if

E[ Mt | ℱs ] = Ms   for all s ≤ t

In words: your best guess of any future value is today's value. No drift, no trend, no exploitable tilt — a fair game. (The two cousins: a supermartingale has E[M_t|ℱ_s] ≤ M_s, drifting down, which is what an unfair casino game or a strategy paying costs looks like; a submartingale has ≥.)

Test our stock. With p = ½, one step from any node with price S:

E[St+1 | ℱt] = ½(1.1·S) + ½(0.9·S) = S·(1.1 + 0.9)/2 = S·1 = St

So S_t is a martingale under this measure — and the tower property extends the one-step result to any horizon: E[S₃|ℱ₁] = E[E[S₃|ℱ₂]|ℱ₁] = E[S₂|ℱ₁] = S₁. Set the second widget's payoff to the price and you would see every node equal its own price; the smoke test for this lesson asserts precisely that. Note how fragile the property is: with p = 0.6 the one-step expectation becomes S·(0.6·1.1 + 0.4·0.9) = 1.02·S, a 2% upward drift per period, and the martingale property fails. Being a martingale is a joint statement about a process, a filtration, and a measure — change any one of the three and the answer changes.

That fragility is the opportunity, and it is where Q2 is headed. The value p* = (1−d)/(u−d) that makes the discounted stock a martingale is the risk-neutral probability; under it, the discounted value of any derivative is also a martingale, so its price today is a conditional expectation of its future payoff — the 7.475 you computed. Units 015–016 build that claim properly (Girsanov for the measure change, Feynman–Kac for the PDE version). Today you have the machine; soon you get the licence to use it.

Trap — the six ways this material is misused. (1) Treating E[X|ℱ_t] as a number. It is a random variable, ℱ_t-measurable, constant on cells. Every downstream error in Q2 traces back to this one; if a manipulation only makes sense for a constant, you have made it. (2) Confusing conditioning on an event with conditioning on a σ-algebra. E[V|{H··}] = 12.725 is a number attached to one event; E[V|ℱ₁] is the whole plan (12.725 or 2.225). The first is Layer 1, the second is what the notation in every textbook means. (3) Believing bigger information is automatically better research. A finer σ-algebra always lowers the mean squared error in sample (Step E). That is exactly why leakage looks like skill, and why "my model improved" is meaningless until you prove the extra information was genuinely available at decision time. (4) Assuming knowing the price means knowing the path. σ(S₂) ⊊ ℱ₂: the price fuses HT and TH. Conditioning on the wrong (coarser) σ-algebra silently changes the answer — and conditioning on a finer one than you really have is leakage. (5) Reading "probability zero" as "impossible." In continuous models every individual path has probability zero, yet one of them happens. "Almost surely" is a statement about measure, not about possibility. (6) Calling a process a martingale without naming the measure. Our stock is a martingale under p = ½ and a submartingale under p = 0.6. "Prices are martingales" is meaningless until you say under which measure and which filtration — the sloppiness that makes people think risk-neutral pricing predicts real-world returns.

Check your understanding

Common questions (and honest answers)

“I've computed conditional expectations since undergrad. What did σ-algebras actually add?”
Undergraduate conditioning is always on an event or on the value of another random variable — one number out, computed by dividing. Conditioning on a σ-algebra gives you a random variable instead, and that is what lets you write objects like E[V|ℱ_t] that evolve in time and can themselves be processes, hedged, and differentiated. Without it you cannot state the martingale property, the tower property across dates, or "the hedge is adapted." The payoff is not rigour for its own sake; it is that the new object composes. Everything in units 012–020 is built by composing conditional expectations across time.
“Is measure theory used in the actual job, or is it interview theater?”
Both, honestly, and the split is worth knowing. You will rarely write "ℱ_t" in production research code — but you will use the discipline daily: every question of the form "was this quantity knowable at decision time?" is a measurability question, and it is the single most expensive class of bug in the field. The formal machinery is genuinely load-bearing when you price or hedge anything (change of measure, martingale representation), and it is examined in interviews for derivatives-adjacent QR roles. For a pure statistical-arbitrage seat, the conditional-expectation-as-projection view is the part you will use every week; the σ-algebra formalism is the part that makes you precise about leakage.
“Why define conditional expectation by that awkward partial-averaging condition instead of a formula?”
Because the formula (average within a cell, divide by the cell's probability) breaks the moment cells have probability zero, which is every continuous-time model you will ever build. The partial-averaging condition never divides, so it survives intact — and on a tree it provably picks out the same object, as the 6.3625 = 6.3625 check showed. This is a recurring pattern in measure theory: the general definition looks indirect because it is engineered to avoid the one operation that fails in the limit.
“If sets of probability zero can be ignored, why mention them at all?”
Because "ignore" has a precise scope: expectations, prices and hedges are unaffected, but statements about individual paths are not. Two random variables equal almost surely have identical expectations, so conditional expectation is only ever unique "up to a null set" — which is why theorems say almost surely. Practically, it also warns you off a class of nonsense: any strategy whose profit relies on what happens on a measure-zero set of paths (say, the price hitting a level exactly) is not a strategy, it is an artifact.
“Our tree is finite. Doesn't everything change when time is continuous?”
The objects do not change at all — Ω, ℱ_t, P, E[·|ℱ_t], martingale — and neither do the three rules or the projection theorem. What changes is that you can no longer enumerate cells, so you compute with distributions and stochastic integrals instead of sums. That is exactly what units 012–014 build. This is why Shreve's Chapter 2 spends its time on the finite case: the intuitions transfer perfectly and the formalism is the same. If a continuous-time manipulation confuses you later, redraw it as a three-step tree — that habit will serve you for two years.
“Why is the risk-neutral probability ½ here rather than the real-world probability?”
Because with u = 1.1, d = 0.9 and zero rates, ½ is the unique weighting under which the stock is a fair game, and pricing by replication turns out to be equivalent to taking an expectation under precisely that weighting. It is not a claim about how often the market really goes up. This lesson deliberately stops at "register the fact"; the derivation (why replication forces that measure, and what changes when rates are non-zero) is unit 015. If you want the intuition now: the hedge, not the forecast, sets the price — so the price cannot depend on your view of the real probability.

Vocabulary you now own

  • Sample space Ω — the set of all complete futures; one element ω is an entire history, not a state at a moment.
  • σ-algebra ℱ — the collection of events whose "did it happen?" your information can settle; equivalently, a partition of Ω into indistinguishable cells (its atoms).
  • Probability measure P — assigns mass to events, countably additively; (Ω, ℱ, P) is a probability space. Single outcomes may carry zero mass.
  • Almost surely — true except on a set of probability zero (so it can be ignored by any expectation, price, or hedge).
  • Filtration {ℱ_t} — an increasing family of σ-algebras: the growing record of what is known.
  • ℱ_t-measurable / adapted — knowable at time t (constant on ℱ_t's cells). A strategy must be adapted; violating this is leakage.
  • Conditional expectation E[X|ℱ] — the ℱ-measurable random variable that averages X within each cell; equivalently, the one reproducing X's mass on every event of ℱ; equivalently, the least-squares projection of X onto the ℱ-measurable subspace.
  • Tower property — E[E[X|ℱ_t]|ℱ_s] = E[X|ℱ_s] for s ≤ t: a guess about a guess collapses to the coarser guess.
  • Taking out what is known — E[YX|ℱ] = Y·E[X|ℱ] when Y is ℱ-measurable.
  • Martingale — an adapted, integrable process with E[M_t|ℱ_s] = M_s: the best forecast of the future is the present value. Always name the measure.
