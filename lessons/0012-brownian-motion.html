Random Walks & Brownian Motion

Lesson 012 · Constructing the continuous-time price model · ~60 min

Warm up

Last lesson you priced an option by doing arithmetic on a tree with eight outcomes. That tree had three time-steps. Real prices do not move three times; they move all day, at every instant. The job of this lesson is to take the coin-flip tree and let the number of steps go to infinity — and to discover that the object you get in the limit, Brownian motion, has one bizarre property that ordinary calculus cannot handle. That property is the reason the next three lessons exist.

Here is the honest map. First we scale a coin-flip walk correctly (the whole trick is a single √n). Then we write down the four rules that define Brownian motion, in plain words. Then we meet the strangeness: a Brownian path is continuous — you can draw it without lifting the pen — yet it is nowhere smooth, so jagged that it has no slope anywhere. And the numerical fingerprint of that jaggedness, its quadratic variation, is not zero (as it would be for any smooth curve) but exactly the elapsed time t. That one fact, written (dW)² = dt, is the seed of Itô's calculus in Lesson 013.

The promise, made concrete. By the end you will have watched a jagged ±1 coin-flip walk turn into a smooth-looking Brownian path as the steps shrink, and you will have added up the squared wiggles of that path and watched the total lock onto t = 1 while the same sum for a straight line collapses to 0. Nothing here needs measure theory you do not already have from Lesson 011.

Re-warm: the coin-flip walk you already own

In Lesson 011 each period the stock was multiplied by u = 1.1 (up) or d = 0.9 (down). To build Brownian motion we use the same coin flips but add instead of multiply, and we make the two moves symmetric: +1 on heads, −1 on tails. That additive version is called a random walk — literally, a running total of independent ±1 steps, the mathematical picture of a drunkard taking one pace left or right at each tick.

Write the individual steps X₁, X₂, …, each equal to +1 or −1 with probability ½, independently. The position after n steps is their sum:

Mn = X₁ + X₂ + ⋯ + Xn

Two numbers describe where this walk tends to be, and both come straight from Lesson 005 — recalled here so nothing is assumed. The mean (the average, the centre of the spread) of one step is E[Xᵢ] = ½(+1) + ½(−1) = 0, so by linearity E[Mn] = 0: the walk drifts nowhere on average. The variance (the average squared distance from the mean — a measure of how spread out the possibilities are) of one step is Var(Xᵢ) = E[Xᵢ²] − 0² = 1, because Xᵢ² = 1 always. And because the steps are independent, variances add:

Var(Mn) = Var(X₁) + ⋯ + Var(Xn) = n

That last equation is the hinge of the whole lesson, so hold it: after n steps the walk is spread out with variance n, which means its typical distance from the start — the standard deviation, the square root of the variance — is √n. The walk wanders, but only about as far as √n, not n. Cross 100 steps and you are typically about 10 away, not 100. Remember that √ and everything follows.

Takeaway: a symmetric random walk has mean 0 and variance n, so it spreads like √n.

The one trick: scale space by √time

We want a limit as the number of steps n → ∞, packed into a fixed clock — say the interval of real time from 0 to 1. Naively you might just plot Mn and take n huge. That fails twice over: the walk's spread √n runs off to infinity (the picture explodes vertically), and each step still has the fixed jump size 1 (the picture never gets smoother). We need to shrink both, and there is exactly one shrink rate that works.

Put n steps into the time interval [0, 1], so each step takes Δt = 1/n of clock time. To reach clock time t you have taken nt steps. Define the scaled walk by dividing the position by √n:

W(n)t = Mnt / √n

Why divide by √n and not by n? Because variance is the quantity we must keep alive, and dividing a random variable by a constant c divides its variance by c² (variance is in squared units — that is the whole reason the scaling is a square root). Watch it work at clock time t, where the number of steps is nt and so the raw variance is nt:

Var(W(n)t) = Var(Mnt) / (√n)² = nt / n = t

The n cancels exactly. No matter how finely you chop time, the scaled walk at clock time t has variance t — finite, non-zero, and independent of the chopping. Divide by n instead and the variance would be t/n → 0 (the walk collapses to the flat line 0); divide by 1 and it blows up. The square-root scaling is the unique rate that keeps the spread finite as time is made continuous. This is the single most important idea in the lesson, and it is just "variance lives in squared units."

One more gift comes for free. The Central Limit Theorem (Lesson 005: the sum of many small independent shocks, once standardised, is approximately normal — the bell curve) tells us the shape of W(n)t in the limit. A sum of nt independent ±1 steps, divided by √n, is a standardised sum, so as n → ∞ it stops being a lumpy discrete thing and becomes a smooth bell curve: a normal (Gaussian) distribution with mean 0 and variance t, written N(0, t). We did not choose the bell curve; the arithmetic of adding many small independent shocks forced it.

Slide the control below from few steps to many and watch the two failures get repaired at once: the jagged walk stays inside a fixed vertical band (variance held at t) while filling in into a continuous-looking path.

The scaled random walk W(n)t = Mnt/√n over clock time [0, 1], driven by the same kind of up/down coin flips as Lesson 011's tree — now added and rescaled instead of multiplied. Slide n (the number of steps) from 2 to 512. The pale line is the fixed fine-resolution limit; the dark line is the current walk. As n grows the dark walk fills in toward the pale one — the same path seen at finer and finer resolution. Crucially the vertical spread never explodes or collapses: Var(Wt) = t at every n. Its endpoint here is W₁ = −0.71, roughly the ±1 spread a variance of 1 predicts.

Takeaway: divide by √n and the walk keeps variance t and turns Gaussian — that is Brownian motion being born.

Brownian motion, defined in four plain sentences

The limit of that scaled walk has a name — Brownian motion (also the Wiener process, after the mathematician who built it rigorously), written {Wt} for t ≥ 0. Rather than reconstruct the limit every time, mathematicians pin it down by four properties. Each is a plain statement; the symbols come after the words.

(1) It starts at zero. W₀ = 0. The walk begins at the origin, by convention. (To model a price you will later add a starting level and a growth rate on top.)
(2) The future move is fresh — it does not remember the past. Take any two times s < t. The change over that stretch, Wt − Ws (called an increment — just "how much it moved between s and t"), is independent of everything that happened up to time s. In the language of Lesson 011, the increment is independent of the filtration ℱs — the record of what is known by time s. The coin about to be flipped does not care how the earlier flips landed.
(3) Each move is a bell curve whose spread is the elapsed time. That increment is normally distributed with mean 0 and variance equal to the length of the time interval: Wt − Ws ~ N(0, t − s). So a move over 0.25 of time has variance 0.25 and typical size √0.25 = 0.5. Longer waits mean bigger typical moves — but only as the square root of the wait, the √n law from the previous section carried into continuous time.
(4) The path is continuous. t ↦ Wt has no jumps — you can draw the whole graph without lifting the pen. (This rules out sudden gaps; those come back deliberately in Lesson 018, jump-diffusion, when we model crashes.)

That is the entire definition. Properties (2) and (3) together are often stated as one phrase, independent, stationary, Gaussian increments: independent (rule 2), Gaussian (the bell curve in rule 3), and stationary meaning the distribution of a move depends only on how long the interval is (t − s), not on when it happens. A move of length 0.1 looks the same at the start of the day as at the end.

Why finance cares. Brownian motion is the raw material of the continuous-time price model. In Lesson 014 the price itself will be built as St = S₀ · exp(drift + σWt) — a starting level S₀, a steady growth term, and a random term that is a Brownian motion scaled by the volatility σ. Everything you price or hedge in Q2 is a function of this one object. Get Wt right and the rest is bookkeeping.

What one value of W looks like: a worked bell curve

Because Wt = Wt − W₀ ~ N(0, t), a single snapshot of Brownian motion is an ordinary normal random variable, and you can compute with it using only Lesson 005. Take t = 4. Then W₄ ~ N(0, 4): mean 0, variance 4, standard deviation √4 = 2. So:

  • The typical size of W₄ is about 2 (one standard deviation).
  • P(W₄ > 0) = ½ exactly — the bell curve is symmetric about its mean 0.
  • P(−2 < W₄ < 2) ≈ 68%, the "within one standard deviation" rule; within ±4 (two standard deviations) is about 95%.

Now the scaling (self-similarity) property, which is the continuous-time echo of √n and worth seeing once. If you speed up the clock by a factor c and shrink space by √c, Brownian motion looks statistically identical:

Wct has the same distribution as √c · Wt

Read it with numbers: W₄ (spread 2) is distributed like √4 · W₁ = 2·W₁, and W₁ has spread 1 — consistent. Zoom into a Brownian path and it looks like another Brownian path; there is no scale at which it becomes smooth. That refusal to smooth out, no matter how far you zoom, is exactly the property we are about to weaponise.

Brownian motion is a martingale (a slow-lane proof)

Recall the load-bearing word from Lesson 011: a martingale is a process whose best forecast of any future value, given everything known so far, is simply its present value — E[Mt | ℱs] = Ms, a "fair game" with no built-in drift. Brownian motion is the archetype. Let us prove E[Wt | ℱs] = Ws for s ≤ t with every step licensed, in the slow lane.

Step A (split into known + increment). Write Wt = Ws + (Wt − Ws). This is just adding and subtracting Ws; it separates the part you already know at time s from the fresh move.
Step B (linearity). Conditional expectation is linear (Lesson 011), so E[Wt|ℱs] = E[Ws|ℱs] + E[Wt − Ws|ℱs].
Step C (take out what is known). Ws is ℱs-measurable — its value is known at time s — so it comes straight out of the conditioning: E[Ws|ℱs] = Ws (Rule 2, "taking out what is known").
Step D (independence kills the increment's conditioning). By defining property (2), the increment Wt − Ws is independent of ℱs, so conditioning on the past does nothing (Rule 3): E[Wt − Ws|ℱs] = E[Wt − Ws] = 0, the last equality by property (3) (the increment has mean 0).
Step E (conclude). Add Steps C and D: E[Wt|ℱs] = Ws + 0 = Ws. ∎ Brownian motion is a martingale.

Notice this used only the two conditional-expectation rules you drilled last lesson, applied to the two defining properties (independent increments, zero mean). That is the pattern for all of Q2: the hard object is new, but the moves on it are the ones you already own.

A second-order fact you will need: W² − t is also a martingale

Here is a consequence that looks like a curiosity now and turns out to be central. The process Wt² − t is also a martingale. The proof reuses the same split; the one new ingredient is that the increment's squared size is its variance.

E[Wt² | ℱs] = E[(Ws + ΔW)² | ℱs],   ΔW = Wt − Ws
Expand the square: (Ws + ΔW)² = Ws² + 2Ws·ΔW + ΔW². Take E[·|ℱs] of each piece. First, Ws² is known at s, so it stays as is. Second, 2Ws·E[ΔW|ℱs] = 2Ws·0 = 0 (take out the known Ws; the increment averages to zero). Third, E[ΔW²|ℱs] = E[ΔW²] = Var(ΔW) = t − s — the mean squared move is the variance, which property (3) fixes at t − s.

So E[Wt²|ℱs] = Ws² + (t − s), which rearranges to E[Wt² − t | ℱs] = Ws² − s: exactly the martingale property for Wt² − t. Keep the punchline in view — to make Wt² a fair game you must subtract t, because the squared wiggles pile up at rate 1 per unit time. That leftover t is the quadratic variation we meet next, and it is the whole reason Itô calculus differs from ordinary calculus.

The paradox: continuous everywhere, smooth nowhere

Property (4) says the path never jumps. You might expect a jump-free curve to be smooth — to have a well-defined slope (a velocity) at each point, the way a car's position does. Brownian motion violates that expectation spectacularly: it is continuous but nowhere differentiable — there is no instant at which it has a slope. Let us see why with the √t law, no heavy machinery.

A slope is a "rise over run": how far the path moved, divided by how long that took. Over a tiny time Δt, the move ΔW is N(0, Δt), so its typical size is √Δt. The difference quotient — the approximate slope — is therefore of size:

|ΔW| / Δt ≈ √Δt / Δt = 1/√Δt

As Δt → 0 this blows up: 1/√Δt → ∞. The path moves by √Δt, which shrinks to zero (so the path is continuous — small time, small move), but it moves far too much relative to the time taken for a finite slope to exist (so it is not differentiable — the slope explodes). Continuous because √Δt → 0; not smooth because √Δt / Δt → ∞. Both facts are the same √ reading two different ways.

Why this is not a technicality. Ordinary calculus is built on the assumption that over a tiny step, the squared move (Δx)² is negligible compared to the step Δx itself — that is what lets you drop second-order terms in a Taylor expansion. For a smooth curve Δx ≈ (slope)·Δt, so (Δx)² ≈ (slope)²·(Δt)², which is tiny next to Δt. For Brownian motion (ΔW)² ≈ Δt — the same order as the time step, not negligible at all. You cannot drop it. Refuse to drop it and you are forced into a new calculus. That is Lesson 013.

Quadratic variation: add up the squared wiggles

Now the load-bearing new concept of the lesson. Chop the interval [0, t] into m small pieces and add up the squares of the moves across each piece. That total is the quadratic variation, written [W]t — literally "the sum of squared increments, as the pieces get small":

[W]t = lim (as pieces → 0)   Σi (Wti+1 − Wti)²

Before the reveal, commit to a guess. For a smooth curve this sum goes to zero (we will confirm it). Squares of tiny numbers are super-tiny, so intuition screams "zero." What does the Brownian path do?

Why the smooth curve gives zero

Take the straight line f(t) = t on [0, 1], cut into m equal pieces. Each move is Δf = 1/m, each squared move is (1/m)² = 1/m², and there are m of them, so the sum is m · 1/m² = 1/m. As m → ∞ that goes to 0. This is completely general: for any curve with a finite slope, Δf ≈ (slope)·Δt, so Σ(Δf)² ≈ Σ(slope)²(Δt)² ≈ (Δt) · Σ(slope)²Δt → 0 because one factor of Δt is left over and drives the whole thing to zero. Smooth curves have zero quadratic variation. This is the fact ordinary calculus quietly relies on.

Why Brownian motion gives t — the slow-lane derivation

Cut [0, t] into m equal pieces of width Δt = t/m. Each increment ΔWi ~ N(0, Δt), independent of the others. Look at the sum of their squares, Q = Σi (ΔWi)², and ask two questions: what is it on average, and how much does it wobble around that average?

The average is exactly t, at every m. The mean squared move is the variance: E[(ΔWi)²] = Var(ΔWi) = Δt (mean is zero, so "mean of the square" equals "variance"). There are m pieces, so by linearity E[Q] = m · Δt = m · (t/m) = t. The m cancels — the same cancellation as the √n scaling, wearing a different hat.
The wobble around t shrinks to zero. The pieces are independent, so their variances add: Var(Q) = Σ Var((ΔWi)²). For a normal variable of variance Δt, the square has variance 2(Δt)² (a standard Gaussian fact). Summing m of them: Var(Q) = m · 2(Δt)² = m · 2(t/m)² = 2t²/m → 0 as m → ∞. So Q piles up ever more tightly onto its mean.

Put the two together: Q has mean t and a spread that vanishes, so in the limit it is not random at all — it equals t with certainty (almost surely, in the Lesson 011 sense). This is the headline result:

[W]t = t   — the quadratic variation of Brownian motion equals the elapsed time

Read what just happened. The squared wiggles of a continuous path do not vanish; they accumulate at a steady rate of exactly 1 per unit time. The randomness in the path is infinite in the first power (the path length Σ|ΔW| ≈ Σ√Δt → ∞, so a Brownian path is infinitely long) yet perfectly deterministic in the second power (Σ(ΔW)² → t). Slide the control below and watch both facts on one screen.

The same fixed Brownian path, cut into m equal pieces (slide m from 2 to 512). The readout reports two running totals of squared increments. For the Brownian path, Σ(ΔW)² hovers around 1 = t and stops wobbling as the mesh refines (locking to exactly 1.00 at the finest cut); for the smooth line f(t) = t, Σ(Δf)² = 1/m marches straight to 0 (0.50, 0.25, 0.125, …). One picture, the whole contrast: squared wiggles matter for Brownian motion and vanish for anything smooth.

The heuristic you will actually compute with: (dW)² = dt. "[W]t = t" is the honest statement; "(dW)² = dt" is its shorthand for a single infinitesimal step, and it is the rule that will run every Itô calculation. It says: when you expand something in small steps and hit a term with (dW)² in it, do not throw it away as second-order junk — replace it by dt, because that is what the squared wiggles sum to. (And dW·dt and (dt)² are negligible and get dropped, since Δt·√Δt and (Δt)² shrink faster than Δt.) This one substitution is the entire difference between ordinary and stochastic calculus.

Takeaway: [W]t = t, i.e. (dW)² = dt. Squared Brownian increments do not vanish — they accumulate at rate 1. Everything in Lesson 013 is built on this.

Explain it back

What this buys you — and where Brownian motion lies

Three payoffs, and one honest warning, before the checks.

  • Itô's calculus (Lesson 013) is now inevitable. Because (dW)² = dt is not negligible, the ordinary chain rule is wrong for functions of Wt. The correction — an extra ½ · (second derivative) · dt term — is exactly the quadratic variation showing up in a Taylor expansion. You already computed its seed: the leftover t in "Wt² − t is a martingale."
  • Quadratic variation is measurable in real data. Summing squared price moves over a day is called realized variance, and it is how volatility is actually estimated from high-frequency data (Year 1 Q3, Lessons 021–026). [W]t = t is the idealised version of that estimator; the messy real version, with jumps and noise, is a whole research area.
  • Pricing becomes a computation. Once the price is S₀·exp(drift + σWt) (Lesson 014) and Brownian motion is a martingale, the risk-neutral price of an option is an expectation you can evaluate — the continuous-time version of the 7.475 you got on the tree.
The warning (failure mode first, as always). Brownian motion is the first model of a log-price, not a faithful one. Its increments are Gaussian (thin-tailed) and have constant variance — which directly contradicts two of the stylized facts you measured in Lesson 004: real returns have fat tails (crashes far larger than a bell curve allows) and volatility clustering (calm and stormy periods, not a constant spread). So Brownian motion is the tractable skeleton every later model fixes: jumps for the fat tails (Lesson 018), stochastic and clustered volatility (Year 1 Q3). Learn it as the baseline you will spend years improving on — never as the truth.
Trap — the six ways this material is misused. (1) Thinking continuous implies smooth. Brownian paths are continuous and nowhere differentiable; "dW/dt" does not exist, which is precisely why we write dW and never W′(t). (2) Dropping (dW)². In ordinary calculus (Δx)² is negligible; for Brownian motion (ΔW)² ≈ Δt is first-order. Throw it away and every Itô result comes out wrong. (3) Scaling space linearly with time. Moves grow like √t, not t. A move over four times the horizon is twice as big, not four times. Dividing the walk by n instead of √n collapses it to a flat line. (4) Confusing the two "variances." Var(Wt) = t is a statement about the distribution of one snapshot; [W]t = t is a statement about a single path's squared wiggles. They happen to share the value t; they are not the same fact (one is random-then-averaged, the other is per-path and non-random in the limit). (5) Reading Gaussian increments as realistic. They rule out the fat tails and volatility clustering of Lesson 004. Brownian motion under-prices disaster. (6) Forgetting the increment is independent of the past. That independence is what makes Wt a martingale; a model with correlated increments (momentum, mean-reversion) is a different animal and needs a drift term.

Check your understanding

Common questions (and honest answers)

“If a Brownian path has no slope anywhere, how can I simulate one on a computer?”
You never simulate the true continuous object — you simulate it on a grid, exactly the scaled random walk from this lesson. Pick a time step Δt, draw independent N(0, Δt) increments, and add them up: Wt+Δt = Wt + √Δt · Z with Z ~ N(0, 1). That is a valid sample of Brownian motion at those grid points, and it is what the lab does. The nowhere-differentiability lives in the limit of ever-finer grids; on any fixed grid the path is a polyline with perfectly ordinary slopes. This is also why "realized variance" from finite data only approximates [W]t = t.
“Variance t and quadratic variation t — isn't that the same statement twice?”
No, and conflating them is Trap 4. Var(Wt) = t is about many parallel universes: run Brownian motion a thousand times, look at the value at time t, and the spread of those thousand numbers is t. [W]t = t is about one universe: take a single path, add up its squared wiggles along the way, and you get t — with no averaging over universes, and essentially no randomness left. It is a happy coincidence of Gaussian increments that both equal t; for other processes the two numbers differ.
“Why Gaussian? Couldn't the limit have been some other bell-ish shape?”
No — the Gaussian is forced, not chosen. The Central Limit Theorem says that a sum of many small independent shocks, once standardised by its own standard deviation, converges to the normal distribution regardless of the shape of the individual shocks (here ±1 coins). Any construction with independent, finite-variance increments and the √n scaling lands on the same Gaussian limit. That universality is why Brownian motion is the continuous-time random walk, not just a one.
“Is measure theory or this [W]t stuff used on the job, or is it exam theater?”
The formal apparatus you will rarely type; the fact [W]t = t you will use constantly, in three disguises. As a modeller: it is why option prices depend on σ²t (variance), not σt. As a data scientist: realized variance (summed squared returns) is a workhorse volatility estimator, and its bias from microstructure noise is a live problem. As an interviewee for any derivatives-adjacent seat: "what is the quadratic variation of Brownian motion, and why does it force the extra term in Itô's lemma?" is a standard question. The intuition is the asset; the formalism is the receipt.
“The path length is infinite? That can't be physical.”
It is a feature of the idealisation, not of any real price. Because each move is √Δt and there are 1/Δt of them per unit time, the total distance travelled is √Δt · (1/Δt) = 1/√Δt → ∞ as the grid refines. Real prices move on a finite tick grid, so their path length is large but finite. The infinite length is the same phenomenon as nowhere-differentiability — a warning that Brownian motion is a mathematical limit that captures the statistics of price wiggles beautifully while taking the "infinitely fine" story too literally. You use the limit because it makes the algebra clean, and you remember it is a limit.

Vocabulary you now own

  • Random walk — a running total of independent ±1 steps; mean 0, variance n after n steps, so it spreads like √n.
  • Scaled random walk — Mnt/√n; the √n keeps its variance at t as the steps shrink, and its limit is Brownian motion.
  • Brownian motion / Wiener process Wt — the continuous-time limit: starts at 0, has independent Gaussian increments Wt − Ws ~ N(0, t−s), and continuous paths.
  • Increment — a change Wt − Ws over a time interval; for Brownian motion it is independent of the past and depends only on the interval length.
  • Self-similarity (Brownian scaling) — Wct is distributed like √c·Wt; zoom in and it looks like Brownian motion at every scale.
  • Quadratic variation [W]t — the limit of the sum of squared increments; equals t for Brownian motion and 0 for any smooth curve.
  • (dW)² = dt — the working shorthand for [W]t = t: keep (dW)² and replace it by dt; drop dW·dt and (dt)².
  • Nowhere differentiable — no slope exists anywhere, because the difference quotient |ΔW|/Δt ≈ 1/√Δt → ∞.
