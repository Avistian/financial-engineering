Risk-Neutral Pricing & the Girsanov Theorem

Lesson 015 · Why a price is a hedging cost, and how changing the probabilities makes it an average · ~65 min

Warm up

Read this first — how this lesson is paced. Same scaffolding as Lesson 014: every load-bearing equation gets an In plain words note that restates it as one sentence, and a with-real-numbers box that plugs in actual numbers. Everything is built from things you already own: a two-outcome tree you can do on paper (Lesson 011), Itô's lemma (013), and the solved stock SDE (014). The only genuinely new object is a second probability measure living on the same outcomes.

You now own a model of how a stock moves — geometric Brownian motion, dS = μS dt + σS dW, solved last lesson. Today you finally use it for the thing the whole quarter has been building toward: putting a price on a contract whose payoff depends on that stock.

The contract we will price throughout is a European call option. Plain picture: a ticket that lets you buy one share at a fixed price K (the strike) on one fixed future date T — and lets you tear the ticket up if you do not want to. If the share is worth 130 and your ticket lets you buy at 100, you use it and pocket 30. If the share is worth 80, the ticket is worthless and you walk away. So its value on the final date is max(ST − K, 0), written (ST − K)⁺ — "whatever is left over above the strike, but never negative." ("European" just means you may only use it on the final date, not before. "Call" means it lets you buy; a put lets you sell.)

The obvious guess for its price today is "the average of what it will pay, discounted back to today." That guess is wrong, and the reason it is wrong opens up the single most important idea in derivative pricing. Here is the map for the hour. First, why the average payoff is not the price, on a two-outcome example you can do in your head. Then the thing that is the price: the cost of a hedging recipe that manufactures the option out of stock and cash. Then the surprise: that cost can be rewritten as an average after all — provided you use a different set of probabilities, the risk-neutral ones. Then Girsanov's theorem, which does the same trick in continuous time by re-weighting Brownian paths, and hands you the famous Black–Scholes price as a single expectation. We finish on the honest warnings — the biggest of which is that these new probabilities are not forecasts.

Why "average payoff" is not the price

Strip the world down to one period and two outcomes — the same tree you built by hand in Lesson 011. A share trades at S0 = 100 today. By the next date it is either up at 110 or down at 90. Cash earns nothing for now (r = 0, an interest rate of zero — we add interest in a moment). Consider a call struck at K = 100: it pays 10 in the up state and 0 in the down state.

Suppose the real-world probability of the up move is p = 0.7. The "obvious" price is the average payoff: 0.7 × 10 + 0.3 × 0 = 7. Hold that number; we are about to show that anybody who sells you this option for 7 has handed you a gift, and that the real answer is 5 — whatever p happens to be.

Two objections to "average payoff" you can feel already, without any math:

  • You do not get the average; you get one outcome. Averages are what you earn over many independent repetitions. This contract happens once. Whoever sells it carries real risk, and risk normally has a price.
  • Nobody agrees on p. If the price depended on the real-world probability, every trader with a different opinion would quote a different price, and the market could never settle on one. Yet option markets quote tight prices all day. Something is pinning the price that is not anybody's forecast.

Takeaway: "expected payoff, discounted" is a forecast, not a price. We need something that a disagreeing crowd would still all agree on.

Predict before you read on

Before the reveal, commit to an answer. Same tree: 100 → 110 or 90, call struck at 100, zero interest. Suppose sentiment flips overnight and everybody now agrees the up move is far more likely than it was.

Replication: a price is a hedging cost

Here is the move that makes derivative pricing a science instead of an opinion poll. Instead of forecasting the option's payoff, build it out of things that already have prices — shares and cash. If a recipe of shares-plus-cash pays exactly what the option pays in every state of the world, then the option and the recipe are the same asset with two names, so they must cost the same today. That recipe is called a replicating portfolio (plain words: a shopping list of stock and cash that manufactures the option), and this whole argument is called replication.

Let us build it, slowly. Our recipe holds Δ shares (read "delta" — just a number of shares, it can be a fraction) and B in cash. Two unknowns, and — conveniently — two states to match.

Step 1 — write what the recipe is worth in each state. With zero interest, cash just stays B. Up state: the shares are worth 110Δ, so the recipe is worth 110Δ + B. Down state: 90Δ + B.
Step 2 — demand it match the option in both states. The option pays 10 up and 0 down, so we need both of 110Δ + B = 10 and 90Δ + B = 0. Two straight-line equations, two unknowns.
Step 3 — subtract to kill the cash. Subtracting the second equation from the first removes B (it appears identically in both): (110 − 90)Δ = 10 − 0, so 20Δ = 10 and Δ = 0.5. Half a share.
Step 4 — back out the cash. Put Δ = 0.5 into the down equation: 90(0.5) + B = 0 ⟹ 45 + B = 0 ⟹ B = −45. A negative cash position means you borrowed 45. (Sanity check in the up state: 110(0.5) − 45 = 55 − 45 = 10 ✔ — it really does pay the option in both states.)
Step 5 — read off today's cost. Buying half a share costs 50; borrowing 45 gives you 45 back. Net cost today: 0.5 × 100 − 45 = 5.
Price of the call = cost of the replicating recipe = 5
In plain words "Half a share, funded by borrowing 45, turns into exactly the option's payoff in both possible futures. Manufacturing the option costs 5 today, so the option is worth 5 today." Nowhere did the probability p appear — not once in Steps 1–5.

Why must the price equal that cost? Because otherwise there is an arbitrage — plain words: a trade that costs you nothing today, can never lose, and might make money. Free money, in other words. Markets do not leave it lying around for long, so any price that permits it is not a real price.

With real numbers — the free money. Suppose the option really did trade at the "average payoff" price of 7. Sell one option: +7 in your pocket. With 5 of it, build the recipe (buy 0.5 shares for 50, borrow 45). You keep +2 right now. At expiry: if the stock is at 110 your recipe is worth 10 and you owe the option buyer exactly 10 — they cancel. If the stock is at 90 your recipe is worth 0 and you owe 0 — they cancel again. You are left with +2, risk-free, in every state. That is the arbitrage, and it is why 7 cannot be the price. The only price that leaves no free money is 5.

Slide the real-world probability below and watch the two candidate prices behave completely differently.

The price ignores the forecast. The flat green line is the replication cost — the arbitrage-free price. The rising red line is "discounted expected payoff under p," the forecast-based guess. Slide p: the forecast moves, the price does not. They agree at exactly one probability — the dashed p*, which is the whole point of the next section.

Takeaway: the arbitrage-free price is the cost of the hedging recipe that reproduces the payoff. It is a manufacturing cost, not a forecast — which is why traders who disagree about the future still agree on the price.

The probability hiding in the algebra

So far, so probability-free. Now the surprise. Redo the five steps with letters instead of numbers, and a strange thing falls out: the answer can be written as an expected payoff after all — just not under the real probabilities.

General one-period setup, and every symbol defined as it appears: the share starts at S0 and moves to uS0 (up factor u, e.g. 1.1) or dS0 (down factor d, e.g. 0.9). The contract pays Cu in the up state and Cd in the down state. Cash multiplies by R over the period — the growth factor of the risk-free account, R = erΔt, so R = 1 means zero interest and R = 1.02 means 2% over the period. Steps 3–5 from before, in letters:

The hedge ratio. Same subtraction as Step 3: Δ = (Cu − Cd) / (uS0 − dS0). In words: the spread of the option's payoffs divided by the spread of the share's, i.e. how many shares move like one option.
The cash leg. Matching the down state, the recipe's cash must be worth Cd − Δ·dS0 at the end, so today it is that amount divided by the growth factor: B = (Cd − Δ·dS0) / R. (Dividing by R is discounting — converting a future amount into the smaller amount you would need today to grow into it.)
The price, then algebra. Price = ΔS0 + B. Substitute B and pull out 1/R: = (1/R)·[ R·ΔS0 + Cd − Δ·dS0 ] = (1/R)·[ ΔS0(R − d) + Cd ]. Legal because every term is just being regrouped — no assumption used.
Cancel the share price. From the hedge ratio, ΔS0 = (Cu − Cd)/(u − d) (the S0 divides out top and bottom). Substituting: Price = (1/R)·[ (Cu − Cd)·(R − d)/(u − d) + Cd ].
Name the ugly fraction. Write p* = (R − d)/(u − d). Then the bracket is p*(Cu − Cd) + Cd = p*Cu + (1 − p*)Cd — which is exactly the shape of an average with weights p* and 1 − p*.
Price = (1/R)·[ p*·Cu + (1 − p*)·Cd ],    p* = (R − d)/(u − d)
In plain words "The price equals the payoff averaged with the weights p* and 1 − p*, then discounted back one period." The weights came out of algebra on a hedging recipe, not out of anyone's forecast. They are numbers manufactured from the three things you can look up — the up factor, the down factor, and the interest rate.

These weights p* and 1 − p* behave like probabilities: they add to one, and they are both between 0 and 1 precisely when d < R < u — which is itself the no-arbitrage condition. (If R ≥ u, cash beats the share in every state, so you would short the share and hold cash for free money; if R ≤ d, the share beats cash in every state, so you would borrow and buy. Either way, free money.) So: a market with no arbitrage is exactly a market where these weights are a valid probability. That is not a coincidence; it is a theorem we will name at the end.

With real numbers. u = 1.1, d = 0.9. Zero rates (R = 1): p* = (1 − 0.9)/(1.1 − 0.9) = 0.1/0.2 = 0.5, so the price is 0.5 × 10 + 0.5 × 0 = 5 — matching the recipe's cost exactly, as it must. With 2% interest (R = 1.02): p* = (1.02 − 0.9)/0.2 = 0.12/0.2 = 0.6, and the price is (0.6 × 10)/1.02 = 6/1.02 = 5.88. Note p* moved from 0.5 to 0.6 with no change whatsoever in anybody's beliefs about the stock — only the interest rate changed. A weight that moves when the interest rate moves and ignores sentiment is clearly not a forecast.

Why "risk-neutral"? Because the stock becomes a fair game

p* is called the risk-neutral probability. Here is the property that earns it the name. Price the share itself with those weights and discount:

With real numbers. Take R = 1.02, so p* = 0.6. Then (1/1.02)·[0.6 × 110 + 0.4 × 90] = (1/1.02)·[66 + 36] = 102/1.02 = 100 — today's share price, exactly. Try the real-world p = 0.7 instead: (1/1.02)·[0.7 × 110 + 0.3 × 90] = (77 + 27)/1.02 = 101.96 — not 100. Only p* reproduces today's price.

Say that in the language of Lesson 011: under the weights p*, the discounted share price is a martingale — a fair game, a process whose best forecast of tomorrow is exactly where it stands today. (Re-warm: X is a martingale when E[Xnext | what you know now] = Xnow; the classic example is your running total in a fair coin-flip game.) Discounted, because cash grows on its own and we want to compare like with like: S/R strips out that automatic growth.

That is the meaning of "risk-neutral." In the real world investors demand extra expected return for holding a risky share — a risk premium (plain words: the extra average return you insist on before you will hold something that can lose money). Under p*, that extra return is gone: every asset is priced as if it grows at the boring risk-free rate. It is the set of weights that a hypothetical crowd who did not care about risk at all would use. Nobody thinks such a crowd exists. It is a calculating device — the change of variables that turns a hedging problem into an averaging problem.

Paying off Lesson 011's IOU: the 7.475

In Lesson 011 you computed the price of a three-period call as 7.475 using conditional expectation, and I owed you an explanation of why the weighting was ½ and not the real-world probability. Now you can see it. That tree had u = 1.1, d = 0.9, and zero interest, so

p* = (R − d)/(u − d) = (1 − 0.9)/(1.1 − 0.9) = ½

The ½ was never an assumption that up and down are equally likely. It was (R − d)/(u − d) evaluated at those particular numbers — a coincidence of the arithmetic. Change the up factor to u = 1.2 and the risk-neutral weight becomes (1 − 0.9)/(1.2 − 0.9) = 1/3, even though nothing about the real world changed. And the multi-period version needs nothing new: at each node you replicate for one step, which is the same two-equation algebra, so you roll the tree backwards taking p*-weighted averages — exactly the backward averaging you already did. Your 7.475 was a risk-neutral price all along; you just had not been told the name.

Takeaway: p* = (R − d)/(u − d). Under it, the discounted stock is a martingale, and every derivative's price is its discounted p*-average payoff. Real-world probabilities never enter.

What "changing the measure" actually means

We have quietly done something that deserves a proper name and a careful definition, because the continuous-time version is the heart of the lesson. We used two different probability assignments on the same set of outcomes. Lesson 011 built the vocabulary for exactly this: a probability measure is the function that assigns weights to events, and it is a separate object from the outcomes themselves. So we can swap it.

The picture to hold. A change of measure does not change what can happen. The list of possible futures is untouched; each future just gets a different weight. Think of a bag of tickets, one per outcome. Changing the measure does not add, remove, or edit a single ticket — it only changes how many copies of each are in the bag.

The bookkeeping device for "how many copies now versus before" is one random number per outcome: the ratio of the new weight to the old. It has a heavyweight name, the Radon–Nikodym derivative, written Z = dQ/dP. Ignore the name; it is a re-weighting factor.

Z(ω) = Q(ω) / P(ω)   ⟹   EQ[X] = EP[ Z·X ]
In plain words "Z at an outcome is how much more (or less) the new probability Q counts that outcome than the old probability P did. And an average under the new probabilities is the same as an average under the old ones, if you first multiply each value by its re-weighting factor." EQ means "average using the Q weights"; the subscript is not decoration — from here on, an expectation without a named measure is meaningless.
With real numbers. Two outcomes. Real world: P(up) = 0.7, P(down) = 0.3. Risk-neutral: Q(up) = Q(down) = 0.5. Then Z(up) = 0.5/0.7 = 0.714 (the up outcome counts about 29% less under Q) and Z(down) = 0.5/0.3 = 1.667 (the down outcome counts 67% more). Check the re-weighting factors average to one, as they must if Q is to be a probability: EP[Z] = 0.7(0.714) + 0.3(1.667) = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1 ✔. And check the pricing identity on the call payoff X = (10, 0): EQ[X] = 0.5(10) = 5, while EP[Z·X] = 0.7 × 0.714 × 10 = 5 ✔. Same number, two routes.

One condition matters enough to state now, because it will come back as a trap. P and Q must be equivalent: they must agree on what is impossible. Formally, they assign probability zero to the same events; concretely, Z must be strictly positive everywhere — you may re-weight a ticket, but you may not shred it, and you may not print a ticket that was not in the bag. Otherwise you would be changing the set of possible futures, and a price built on a future that cannot happen is worthless. (If Q could set the probability of a crash to zero, "no arbitrage" would stop meaning anything.)

Girsanov: the same trick, on Brownian paths

Now the continuous-time version. Under the real-world measure P, our stock follows dS = μS dt + σS dW: it drifts up at rate μ and is shoved around by a Brownian motion W. We want a new measure Q under which the drift is the risk-free rate r instead, because — as the binomial showed — that is the measure in which discounted prices are fair games and prices become averages.

In the two-outcome world we just re-weighted two tickets. Here there are infinitely many tickets: one per Brownian path. Girsanov's theorem is the recipe for that re-weighting. Its statement, with every symbol defined:

Girsanov (constant-shift version). Let W be a Brownian motion under P on [0, T], and pick any number θ ("theta" — the size of the shift you want). Define the re-weighting factor ZT = exp(−θWT − ½θ²T) and let Q be the measure that weights each path by it. Then Q is a genuine probability measure equivalent to P, and the shifted process W̃t = Wt + θt is a Brownian motion under Q.
In plain words "Weight each path by an exponential of where its Brownian shock ended: paths that finished high count for less, paths that finished low count for more (when θ is positive). Under those new weights, the old Brownian motion plus a steady slope θt is itself a perfectly ordinary Brownian motion." The −½θ²T is not a modelling choice — it is exactly the amount that makes the new weights average to one, i.e. makes Q a probability rather than something that sums to more or less than 100%.

Two things deserve checking rather than accepting, and both are one line with tools you already have.

Check 1 — the weights really do average to one. Re-warm from Lesson 013 (your lab confirmed it numerically): E[eaWT] = e½a²T for any constant a, because WT is normal with mean 0 and variance T. Apply it with a = −θ: EP[ZT] = EP[e−θWT]·e−½θ²T = e½θ²T·e−½θ²T = 1 ✔. That is the entire job of the −½θ² term.
Check 2 — the shift really is all that happens. Under P, WT has the bell-curve density φ(w) = (1/√(2πT))·e−w²/(2T). Under Q its density is that, multiplied by the re-weighting factor. Collect the exponents: −θw − ½θ²T − w²/(2T). Factor out −1/(2T): −(1/2T)·[ w² + 2θTw + θ²T² ]. The bracket is a perfect square — (w + θT)² — which is the schoolbook trick of completing the square (x² + 2bx + b² = (x + b)², here with b = θT). So the Q-density of WT is (1/√(2πT))·e−(w + θT)²/(2T): still a bell curve, still of width √T, but centred at −θT instead of 0. Shifting it back — that is exactly W̃ = W + θt — recentres it at zero. The mean moved; the width did not. ✔
With real numbers. Take θ = 0.5 and T = 1, so Z = exp(−0.5·W1 − 0.125). A path whose Brownian shock ended at W1 = +1 gets weight e−0.625 = 0.54 — it counts about half as much as before. A path that ended at W1 = −1 gets e+0.375 = 1.46 — nearly one and a half times as much. A path that ended at 0 gets e−0.125 = 0.88. Averaged over all paths these come to exactly 1, and the effect is to slide the whole bell curve of W1 down by θT = 0.5 without changing its width.

The market price of risk: how big a shift do we need?

Girsanov gives us a shift of any size we like. Which size turns the real drift μ into the risk-free rate r? Substitute and see. From W̃ = W + θt we get dW̃ = dW + θ dt, so dW = dW̃ − θ dt. Feed that into the stock's SDE:

Substitute. dS = μS dt + σS dW = μS dt + σS(dW̃ − θ dt).
Collect the dt terms. dS = (μ − σθ)S dt + σS dW̃. The random part is untouched: still σS, just measured against the new Brownian motion.
Demand the drift be r. Set μ − σθ = r and solve: σθ = μ − r, hence θ = (μ − r)/σ.
θ = (μ − r) / σ   ⟹   dS = rS·dt + σS·dW̃  (under Q)
In plain words "The shift you need is the stock's extra return over cash, divided by its volatility — that is, the extra reward per unit of risk. Apply it and the stock's average growth becomes the cash rate, while its wobble stays exactly σ."

That quantity θ = (μ − r)/σ has a name you should hold onto: the market price of risk, also called the Sharpe ratio of the asset. Read it literally: the numerator μ − r is how much more you expect to earn than by leaving the money in cash (the risk premium); the denominator σ is how much you get shoved around while earning it. The ratio is reward per unit of risk — and it is the single most quoted number in your future job, because a strategy's Sharpe ratio is exactly this quantity computed on the strategy's returns.

With real numbers. A stock with expected return μ = 15%, volatility σ = 20%, cash at r = 5%: θ = (0.15 − 0.05)/0.20 = 0.5. So the risk-neutral world is reached by sliding the Brownian motion by half a unit per year. Under Q the same stock grows at 5%, not 15% — the 10% risk premium has been re-weighted away. A more exciting stock with μ = 25% and the same volatility needs a bigger shift, θ = 1.0. A stock that earns exactly the cash rate needs no shift at all (θ = 0): it is already risk-neutral.

And because Girsanov only re-weights, the picture below is the honest one: nobody moves the paths. The slider changes the real-world drift μ; watch the weights redistribute so the weighted average always lands on the cash-rate curve.

Same paths, different weights. Faint lines are simulated stock paths — identical at every slider position. Each is drawn darker and thicker when the risk-neutral measure counts it more (weight Z = e−θW−½θ²t). The solid curve is the ordinary average, growing at μ; the dashed curve is the weighted average, which sits on S0ert for every μ you choose. Slide μ up: the paths do not budge; only who counts changes.

Finally, the solved form. Under Q the stock is still a geometric Brownian motion, so Lesson 014's solve applies verbatim with μ replaced by r and W replaced by W̃:

ST = S0·exp( (r − ½σ²)T + σW̃T )  (under Q)
In plain words "In the risk-neutral world the stock still compounds and still wiggles by σ, but its average growth rate is the cash rate r — and it still carries the −½σ² volatility drag from Lesson 013, because that term comes from the curvature of the logarithm, which no change of measure can touch."

Takeaway: Girsanov shifts a Brownian motion by θt by re-weighting paths. Choosing θ = (μ − r)/σ — the market price of risk — turns the stock's drift into r and leaves σ alone.

Why the volatility survives but the drift does not

This deserves its own section, because it is the deepest idea in the lesson and it will shape how you think about research for years.

A change of measure can move the drift anywhere it likes. It cannot touch the volatility. Here is the reason, and you already proved the key fact in Lesson 012: the quadratic variation of Brownian motion is [W]t = t — add up the squared wiggles on a fine grid and you get t, not approximately but in the limit, on essentially every path. It is a property of the path itself, not of an average across paths.

Now recall the equivalence requirement: P and Q agree about what is impossible. A statement that is true on all paths except a set of probability zero under P is therefore also true on all paths except a set of probability zero under Q. Quadratic variation is such a statement. So it is the same under both measures — and since the stock's squared wiggles accumulate at rate σ²S², the volatility you would measure from a single price path is the same in both worlds.

In plain words "Volatility is something you can read off one path by measuring how much it jiggles. Re-weighting which paths count cannot change how much a given path jiggles — so volatility is not up for negotiation. Drift, by contrast, is only visible by averaging across many possible futures, and that is exactly what re-weighting changes."

That asymmetry is not a curiosity — it is the statistical shape of your entire future job:

With real numbers — why drift is the hard one. Estimating volatility gets better the more finely you sample: a year of daily data gives roughly 252 observations of the wiggle, a year of minute bars gives about 100,000, and the estimate tightens with each. Estimating the drift does not work that way. The uncertainty in an estimate of μ from a single price history is σ/√T, where T is the calendar length of the history — sampling more often within the same window does not help at all, because the extra observations add the same drift signal and the same noise in proportion. With σ = 20%, to pin the drift down to ±1% you would need T = (0.20/0.01)² = 400 years of data. This is why Q1 was all statistical hygiene, why a "great" backtest is usually noise, and why quant researchers who claim to know expected returns to the basis point are selling something.

The risk-neutral pricing formula

Put the pieces together. In the binomial, replication forced the price to be a discounted average under p*. The same logic runs in continuous time, and gives the formula the rest of the quarter uses:

Vt = EQ[ e−r(T−t)·VT  |  ℱt ]
In plain words "The value today of a contract that pays VT at time T is: average that payoff using the risk-neutral weights, given everything known so far, then shrink it back to today's money by the risk-free discount factor." ℱt is the information available at time t — the filtration from Lesson 011 — so this is a conditional expectation, the exact object you computed on a tree.

Equivalently, and more memorably: under Q, every discounted price is a martingale. You can verify that for the stock itself in three lines with Itô, and it is a clean re-use of Lesson 013's "linear function ⇒ no correction" rule. Let f(t, S) = e−rtS — the discounted price.

Derivatives of f. ft = −re−rtS (differentiate the exponential in t), fs = e−rt, and fss = 0 because f is a straight line in S.
Itô's lemma with the Q-coefficients (a = rS, b = σS): the drift bracket is ft + a·fs + ½b²·fss = −re−rtS + rS·e−rt + 0 = 0. The two terms are equal and opposite — they cancel exactly.
What is left. d(e−rtS) = σe−rtS·dW̃ — pure dW̃, no dt. An integral of … dW̃ is a martingale (Lesson 013), so the discounted stock price is a fair game under Q. ✔

One honest gap, named rather than hidden. In the binomial, "the price equals the discounted Q-average" followed from a replicating recipe you could write down. In continuous time you need to know that a replicating strategy still exists — that any Q-martingale built on this Brownian motion can be written as an Itô integral, i.e. as the running profit of some stock-and-cash trading strategy. That is the martingale representation theorem (Shreve II §5.3): the continuous-time guarantee that "you can always build it," which is what licenses the pricing formula. We use it; Lesson 017 makes the hedging strategy concrete when you actually delta-hedge an option and watch the profit and loss.

Black–Scholes as a single expectation

Now cash in. Price a European call under Q, where ST = S0exp((r − ½σ²)T + σW̃T) and W̃T is normal with mean 0 and variance T:

C = EQ[ e−rT·(ST − K)⁺ ] = S0·N(d1) − K·e−rT·N(d2)
d1 = [ ln(S0/K) + (r + ½σ²)T ] / (σ√T),    d2 = d1 − σ√T

That is the Black–Scholes formula, and notice what just happened: it is not a new theory. It is one integral — the average of a hockey-stick payoff against a bell curve — written out. N(·) is the standard normal cumulative distribution function from Lesson 005: N(x) is the probability that a standard bell-curve draw lands below x. The algebra that turns the integral into these two terms is a page of completing-the-square (you do it numerically in the lab, and Lesson 016 re-derives the same price from a differential equation instead).

In plain words "The call is worth the share you might receive, minus the cash you might have to pay for it — each counted only in the worlds where the deal actually happens. N(d2) is the risk-neutral chance you end up exercising, so K·e−rT·N(d2) is the discounted strike you expect to pay. S0·N(d1) is the value of the share you expect to receive — weighted a little more heavily than a plain probability, because the worlds where you exercise are also the worlds where the share is worth more."
With real numbers. S0 = 100, K = 100, r = 5%, σ = 20%, T = 1 year. Then ln(100/100) = 0, so d1 = (0 + (0.05 + 0.02)·1)/(0.2·1) = 0.35 and d2 = 0.35 − 0.2 = 0.15. From the normal table N(0.35) = 0.6368, N(0.15) = 0.5596, and e−0.05 = 0.9512. So C = 100(0.6368) − 100(0.9512)(0.5596) = 63.68 − 53.23 = 10.45. A one-year at-the-money call on a 20%-volatility stock costs about a tenth of the share price. Commit that magnitude to memory; it is a standard interview sanity check.

And the promised continuity: the binomial tree you priced by hand is Black–Scholes, at a coarse resolution. Chop the year into n steps with up factor u = eσ√Δt and d = 1/u (so the tree has the right volatility), use p* = (erΔt − d)/(u − d), and let n grow.

The tree becomes the formula. Each red dot is the price from an n-step risk-neutral tree; the dashed line is the Black–Scholes value 10.451. Slide n: one step gives 12.16 (a two-outcome caricature of the world), and the dots zig-zag — odd and even step counts straddle the answer differently — while closing in. This is the whole lesson in one picture: same risk-neutral logic, finer grid.

Explain it back

The two fundamental theorems (in plain words)

Everything above is a special case of two results that carry an intimidating name — the fundamental theorems of asset pricing — and a very simple meaning.

TheoremStatementWhat it means for you
FTAP 1 There is no arbitrage if and only if there exists at least one equivalent measure Q under which discounted prices are martingales. "No free money" and "a risk-neutral measure exists" are the same statement. If you cannot find a Q, your model contains free money — a bug, not an edge.
FTAP 2 The market is complete — every payoff can be replicated by trading — if and only if that measure Q is unique. One Q ⇒ one price, forced. Many Q's ⇒ a range of arbitrage-free prices, and the market picks one for reasons beyond no-arbitrage.

Our binomial and Black–Scholes worlds are complete: two states and two instruments, or one Brownian motion and one stock — enough building blocks to manufacture anything. Reality is not so tidy. Add jumps (Lesson 018) or make volatility itself random and there are more sources of risk than there are things to trade with, so replication fails, many Q's survive, and no-arbitrage alone no longer pins a single price. Practitioners then choose a Q by calibrating to the options that already trade. Worth knowing early: much of what looks like "pricing theory" on a desk is really the choice of which Q to believe.

What this buys you

  • Every pricer you will write. Monte-Carlo option pricing is one line of this lesson: simulate ST under Q (drift r, not μ), average the payoff, discount. That is Lesson 019 and the Lesson 020 checkpoint.
  • Reading the market backwards. Because σ is the one parameter that survives the measure change, the market's option prices can be inverted for it — the implied volatility you will meet in the volatility track. Prices do not tell you the drift, and now you know precisely why they can't.
  • A permanent guard rail. Real-world P for forecasting and research; risk-neutral Q for pricing and hedging. Mixing them up produces confident nonsense — the failure mode below.
  • The Sharpe ratio, understood. θ = (μ − r)/σ is not just a scorecard for strategies; it is literally the size of the shift between the real world and the pricing world.
The honest warning (failure mode first, as always). Risk-neutral pricing is exact given the model, and the model is a fiction in the familiar ways. It assumes you can hedge continuously, at no cost, in any size, with constant volatility and no jumps. Every one of those is false: hedging is discrete and costs money, volatility clusters and moves (Lesson 004), and prices gap. The formula's answer is exactly right for a world we do not live in. That is still enormously useful — it is the reference point every trading desk quotes against — but "exact given the model" is never "true."
Trap — the seven ways this material bites. (1) Reading risk-neutral probabilities as forecasts. With μ = 15%, σ = 20%, r = 5%, our one-year call has N(d2) = 56% risk-neutral chance of finishing in the money, but the real-world chance is N(0.65) = 74%. Both are correct answers to different questions. Quoting a "risk-neutral probability of default" as if it were a default forecast is the same error, and it is made professionally, often. (2) Believing Girsanov can change volatility. It cannot — quadratic variation is a path-by-path fact and equivalent measures agree on path-by-path facts. Only the drift moves. (3) Simulating with μ instead of r. The single most common pricing bug: a Monte-Carlo pricer fed the real-world drift returns a number that is not a price. The lab makes you produce the wrong answer on purpose so you recognise it. (4) Forgetting to discount. EQ[VT] is a future average, not a price; the price is e−rTEQ[VT]. (5) Thinking "risk-neutral" describes investors. Nobody is risk-neutral. The risk premium has not vanished — it has been absorbed into θ, and it still governs what you actually earn. (6) Assuming Q exists and is unique. Existence is no-arbitrage (FTAP 1), uniqueness is completeness (FTAP 2). With jumps or stochastic volatility, many Q's survive and no-arbitrage alone will not give you one price. (7) Breaking equivalence. A change of measure may re-weight outcomes but never create or destroy them (Z > 0 everywhere). Any argument that "under Q the crash cannot happen" has left pricing theory behind.

Check your understanding

Common questions (and honest answers)

“If the real probability doesn't matter, does that mean my forecast is worthless?”
No — it means your forecast is worthless for pricing a replicable derivative, which is a narrow claim. The real-world measure P is exactly where your job lives: expected returns, Sharpe ratios, portfolio construction, and every backtest are P-world questions. What this lesson says is that the option's price is pinned by the hedging cost, so a view on p shows up in your profit, not in the quote. If you think the stock will rise, buy the stock (or the option, and hedge) — but do not expect the option's fair value to agree with you.
“Where did the risk premium go? It feels like it was smuggled out.”
It went into θ, and it is still there in the real world. The reason it can be re-weighted away for pricing purposes is that the hedged portfolio does not earn it: if you hold the option and short Δ shares, you have removed exactly the exposure that the risk premium pays you for. What is left is a riskless position, and a riskless position can only earn r without creating free money. So the premium is absent from the price for the same reason it is absent from a bank deposit.
“Why must the volatility be the same under both measures — could a different Q not just scale it?”
Because σ is determined by each path individually, through the quadratic variation [W]t = t, and equivalent measures cannot disagree about a property that holds on almost every path. A measure change that scaled volatility would have to make paths with the old wiggle-rate impossible and paths with a new one possible — i.e. it would not be equivalent, and would break the no-arbitrage machinery it was meant to support. Practically: if your calibrated "risk-neutral volatility" differs from realised volatility, that is a statement about your model or about a volatility risk premium, never about Girsanov.
“The martingale representation theorem was waved at rather than proved. How much should that bother me?”
Enough to know exactly what it buys and where to find it, not enough to stall. It is the continuous-time replacement for "solve two equations for Δ and B": it guarantees the hedging strategy exists, so the discounted-expectation formula really is a price and not just a number. The statement and proof are Shreve II §5.3. Lesson 017 makes it operational — you compute the hedge ratio Δ, run it discretely, and see the leftover profit and loss that the theorem promises would vanish in the continuous limit.
“Why bother with the binomial at all, if Black–Scholes is a formula?”
Three reasons. It is where the logic is visible — you can see the replication and the p* with a pencil. It generalises where the formula does not: American options (early exercise) and exotic payoffs are routinely priced on trees precisely because you can inspect the value at every node. And it is the cleanest demonstration that Black–Scholes is not an extra assumption but a limit — which is what the convergence picture and the lab make concrete.
“Is θ the same for every stock, or does each have its own?”
In this one-stock, one-Brownian-motion world there is exactly one θ, and it is forced. With several assets driven by shared risk factors, no-arbitrage forces every asset exposed to a given factor to carry the same market price of risk for that factor — otherwise you could go long the generous one and short the stingy one and pocket the difference risklessly. That constraint is the continuous-time ancestor of factor models: assets differ in how much factor risk they carry, not in the price paid per unit of it. You will meet it again in the factor-model track.

Vocabulary you now own

  • European call / put — a ticket to buy (call) or sell (put) one share at a fixed strike K on a fixed date T; the call pays (ST − K)⁺.
  • Arbitrage — a trade costing nothing today that can never lose and might win. Any price permitting one is not a price.
  • Replicating portfolio / replication — a recipe of shares and cash that reproduces a payoff in every state; its cost is the arbitrage-free price.
  • Hedge ratio Δ — shares per option in the recipe, (Cu − Cd)/(Su − Sd).
  • Risk-neutral probability p* — (R − d)/(u − d); the weights under which the discounted stock is a martingale. Not a forecast.
  • Change of measure — same outcomes, different weights; formalised by the Radon–Nikodym derivative Z = dQ/dP with EQ[X] = EP[ZX].
  • Equivalent measures — P and Q agree on what is impossible (Z > 0 everywhere).
  • Girsanov's theorem — weighting paths by ZT = e−θWT − ½θ²T makes W̃t = Wt + θt a Brownian motion under Q.
  • Market price of risk (Sharpe ratio) θ — (μ − r)/σ: extra return over cash per unit of volatility; the size of the drift shift.
  • Risk-neutral pricing formula — Vt = EQ[e−r(T−t)VT | ℱt]; equivalently, discounted prices are Q-martingales.
  • Black–Scholes formula — C = S0N(d1) − Ke−rTN(d2), the risk-neutral expectation evaluated; N(d2) is the Q-chance of exercise.
  • Martingale representation theorem — every Q-martingale on this Brownian motion is an Itô integral, i.e. the profit of some trading strategy: replication exists.
  • FTAP 1 / FTAP 2 — no arbitrage ⟺ a Q exists; completeness ⟺ that Q is unique.

Where you are in the arc

Lesson 014 gave you a solved model of how a stock moves. Today you learned that a price is not a forecast of that motion but the cost of manufacturing the payoff — and that this cost can be rewritten as an average, provided you re-weight the futures so that the stock earns only the cash rate. In the two-outcome world that re-weighting is the single number p* = (R − d)/(u − d); in continuous time it is Girsanov, shifting the Brownian motion by θt with θ = (μ − r)/σ. The drift moves; the volatility cannot. And the whole of Black–Scholes falls out as one expectation.
