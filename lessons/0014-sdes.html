Stochastic Differential Equations: GBM & Ornstein–Uhlenbeck

Lesson 014 · How random quantities evolve — and how to solve for them · ~60 min

So far you have met one random object (Brownian motion, Lesson 012) and one rule for functions of it (Itô's lemma, Lesson 013). Today we use them to describe how a real quantity changes over time when part of the change is random — a stock price, an interest rate, the gap between two related stocks. The description is called a stochastic differential equation, or SDE for short. "Stochastic" is just a fancy word for "random"; "differential equation" means "a rule for how something changes over a tiny step." Put together: an SDE is a rule for how a random quantity nudges forward, one tiny step at a time.

Here is the honest map for the hour. First, what an SDE actually is, next to the ordinary (non-random) equations you already know. Then the one trick that solves the two SDEs that can be solved by hand — and you already own the trick, it is just Itô's lemma pointed the right way. We solve the stock model (geometric Brownian motion) you half-saw last lesson, then the mean-reverting model (Ornstein–Uhlenbeck) that underlies pairs trading and interest-rate models — the first genuinely new object of the day. We close on a plain-English version of a deep question: when does an SDE even have a solution, and only one?

What an SDE is (next to equations you already know)

Start from something with no randomness at all. An ordinary differential equation (ODE) is a rule that says "over a tiny time step dt, the quantity x changes by this much." For example, money in a bank account at continuous interest r grows by dx = r·x·dt: the change is "rate times balance times the length of the step." Given a starting balance, the future is completely determined — run it twice and you get the exact same curve, x_t = x_0 e^{rt}.

An SDE adds one thing: a random shove on every step, built from the Brownian motion W of Lesson 012. We write the general one-dimensional SDE as

dX = a(X, t)·dt  +  b(X, t)·dW
Say it in words. "Over the next tiny step, X moves by two things added together: a steady push a·dt — the direction it is pulled, times the length of the step — plus a random shove b·dW — a fresh coin-flip-sized kick whose typical size is b." The steady push a is called the drift; the shove size b is called the diffusion (or volatility). Both may depend on where X currently is and on the time.

The random shove is what makes it an SDE and not an ODE. Run an SDE twice and you get two different paths, because dW is a fresh random draw each time (mean 0, variance dt — Lesson 012). "Solving" an SDE therefore cannot mean "find the one curve." It means one of two things, and we will do both:

  • A closed-form solution: a formula for X_t written directly in terms of W_t (and t), so you can read off its average, its spread, and simulate it in one line. Only special SDEs have one — the two in this lesson do.
  • A simulation: take the SDE literally as a recipe and step it forward on a computer (X_{next} = X_{now} + a·dt + b·√dt·(a standard normal draw)). This always works; it is the whole of the lab.

Takeaway: an SDE is an ODE plus a random shove b·dW. Drift a = the steady pull; diffusion b = the size of the random kick. "Solving" means a formula for X_t, or a step-by-step simulation.

Re-warm: Itô's lemma in one line (we are about to lean on it)

Everything today rests on one result from Lesson 013. Let us restate it so it is fresh, with nothing assumed. If X follows an Itô process dX = a·dt + b·dW, and f(t, X) is a smooth function of time and of X, then f itself moves according to

df = ( ft + a·fx + ½ b²·fxx ) dt  +  b·fx·dW
Say it in words. "How f changes = [ how f drifts with time on its own (ft), plus the process's drift carried through the slope (a·fx), plus the curvature correction (½ b²·fxx) ] times dt, plus a random part of size b·fx times dW." Here ft, fx, fxx are just "how f changes with time," "…with X," and "how that slope itself bends."

Two facts from 013 we will use again and again:

  • The dW part averages to zero — any ∫ … dW is a martingale, a fair game with no built-in trend. So all the average drift lives in the dt bracket.
  • The curvature term ½ b² fxx is zero when f is a straight line in X (because then fxx = 0). Hold onto this — it is exactly why the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck solve below is clean.

The one trick for solving an SDE

Here is the whole idea, in one sentence, and it is the sentence to remember from this lesson:

The trick. To solve an SDE for X, find a function of X whose Itô expansion has no X left on the right-hand side — only t and dW. Once the right side is free of X, you can just add up (integrate) the steps directly, because there is nothing left that depends on where you are.

Why does that help? An SDE like dX = a(X)dt + b(X)dW is hard because the push and the shove depend on the current X — a feedback loop. If we can find a cleverly chosen function Y = f(t, X) whose change dY has the X cancelled out, then Y is easy: its steps no longer refer to themselves, so summing them is ordinary integration. We solve for Y, then undo f to get X. The only art is choosing f. For our two SDEs the right choices are famous: log S for the stock, and eθtX for the mean-reverter.

Worked solve 1: geometric Brownian motion (the stock)

The standard model of a stock price says its percentage moves are Brownian — a fixed average growth plus a random wiggle proportional to the current price (so the price cannot go negative). This is geometric Brownian motion (GBM):

dS = μS·dt + σS·dW   (drift a = μS, diffusion b = σS)
Say it in words. "Over the next step, the price S moves by: average growth rate μ times the current price times the step, plus volatility σ times the current price times a random kick." Both terms scale with S, which is why a \$500 stock and a \$5 stock can have the same percentage behaviour.

Apply the trick: which function of S kills the S on the right? The answer is log S (the natural logarithm), because dividing by S is exactly what its slope does. Let f(S) = log S. Re-warm the calculus: the slope of log S is fx = 1/S, and the slope of 1/S is fxx = −1/S²; there is no direct time dependence, so ft = 0. Feed these into Itô's lemma, slow lane, one line at a time:

Drift bracket. ft + a·fx + ½ b²·fxx = 0 + (μS)·(1/S) + ½·(σS)²·(−1/S²). Now cancel the S's: μS/S = μ, and ½·σ²S²·(−1/S²) = −½σ². So the drift is μ − ½σ² — free of S. ✔ The trick worked.
Random part. b·fx = (σS)·(1/S) = σ — also free of S. So the dW term is just σ·dW.
d(log S) = (μ − ½σ²)·dt + σ·dW

The right-hand side has no S in it — only constants and dW. So we can add up the steps from 0 to t directly. The dt pieces add to (μ − ½σ²)·t; the σ dW pieces add to σ·Wt (that is just what "sum of the shocks" means, since here the position σ is constant). So:

log St − log S0 = (μ − ½σ²)·t + σ·Wt  ⟹  St = S0·exp( (μ − ½σ²)·t + σ·Wt )

That closed form is the solved GBM SDE. It is the formula every Monte-Carlo stock simulator uses — and the −½σ² in the exponent is the Itô correction you derived last lesson. Drop it and your simulated prices drift too high. The picture below is the same volatility-drag widget from Lesson 013, here as a reminder that the mean price grows at μ while the typical (median) path grows only at μ − ½σ².

The solved stock SDE. Faint lines are simulated St paths from the closed form. The solid mean grows at μ; the dashed median grows at μ − ½σ². Slide σ: more volatility, bigger gap — the drag ½σ².

Takeaway: GBM solves to St = S0·exp((μ − ½σ²)t + σWt), found by applying Itô to log S — the function that cancels the S.

Predict before you read on

Now the new model. A stock wanders: nothing pulls it back to where it started, and on average it grows forever. But many quantities are different — they get pulled back toward a normal level whenever they stray. The gap between two related stocks, an interest rate, a commodity's inventory-driven price: push them away and a force drags them home. Before we write the equation, predict what such a process does in the long run.

The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process (mean reversion)

Picture a rubber band, or a spring. Tie one end to a fixed post at level m and the other to your quantity X. Whenever X is above m, the band pulls it down; whenever it is below, the band pulls it up; and the pull is stronger the further away it is. Meanwhile the world keeps giving it random shoves. That is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process — the simplest "pulled-back-to-normal," or mean-reverting, random process. Its SDE:

dX = θ(m − X)·dt + σ·dW
Say it in words. "Over the next step, X moves by: the pull strength θ times how far it is below the target (m − X) times the step — plus a random shove of size σ." Three plain-English knobs:
  • m — the target level ("where the post is"): the value it is pulled toward. Traders call it the long-run mean.
  • θ — the pull strength ("how stiff the spring is"; read "theta"): big θ snaps back fast, small θ drifts home lazily.
  • σ — the shove size ("how hard the world buffets it"): the random volatility.

Look at the drift term θ(m − X) and notice how it produces the rubber-band behaviour, no algebra needed. If X > m then m − X is negative, so the drift is negative — it pushes X down, back toward m. If X < m then m − X is positive and the drift pushes X up. Right at X = m the drift is zero — no pull, just the random shoves. That single sign flip is the whole of mean reversion.

With real numbers. Take θ = 2, m = 100, and suppose right now X = 80 (below target). The steady pull this instant is θ(m − X) = 2·(100 − 80) = +40 per unit time — a strong upward tug. If instead X = 130 (above target), the pull is 2·(100 − 130) = −60 per unit time — downward, and stronger, because it strayed further. The rubber band always points home, and pulls harder the further out you are.

Watch it happen. Below, many OU paths start at X0 = 80 and are pulled toward m = 100. The slider is the pull strength θ.

Mean reversion, live. Faint lines are OU paths from X0 = 80; the solid curve is the average, bending up toward m = 100; the dashed band is the long-run spread m ± √(σ²/2θ). Slide θ up: the average snaps to m faster and the band tightens — a stiffer spring reverts quicker and holds closer.

Worked solve 2: solving OU (the integrating-factor trick)

Can we get a closed form for OU too? Yes — same trick, different function. The X we want to cancel sits inside the drift θ(m − X). The classic move (borrowed straight from how you solve the non-random version of this ODE) is to multiply by a growing factor eθt — an integrating factor — chosen so the awkward −θX term cancels. So try the function

f(t, X) = eθt·X

Re-warm its derivatives. As a function of X it is a straight line (just X times something), so: fx = eθt and fxx = 0. As a function of time, ft = θeθt·X. Because fxx = 0, the whole Itô correction vanishes — this is where last lesson's "linear function ⇒ no correction" fact pays off. Now feed Itô's lemma with the OU coefficients a = θ(m − X) and b = σ, one line at a time:

Write the drift bracket. ft + a·fx + ½ b²·fxx = θeθtX + θ(m − X)·eθt + ½·σ²·0.
Expand the middle term. θ(m − X)eθt = θm·eθt − θX·eθt. Substitute back: θeθtX + θm·eθt − θX·eθt.
Cancel. The first and third terms are +θeθtX and −θeθtX — they cancel exactly. The X is gone. ✔ What survives is just θm·eθt.
Random part. b·fx = σ·eθt. So altogether:
d( eθtX ) = θm·eθt·dt + σ·eθt·dW

The right side has no X — only t and dW. Add up the steps from 0 to t. The dt piece: θm·∫0t eθsds = θm·(eθt − 1)/θ = m(eθt − 1) (ordinary calculus: the integral of eθs is eθs/θ). The dW piece stays as an Itô integral, σ∫0teθsdWs. And the left side added up is eθtXt − e0X0 = eθtXt − X0. Put it together and divide both sides by eθt:

Xt = X0·e−θt + m·(1 − e−θt) + σ·∫0t e−θ(t−s) dWs

Takeaway: OU solves to three pieces — a fading memory of the start X0e−θt, a pull toward the target m(1 − e−θt), and a running noise term with mean zero. Found by applying Itô to the straight-line function eθtX (so no correction term).

Reading the OU solution (what those three pieces mean)

A formula you cannot read is useless, so let us read each piece in plain words, then get its average and its spread.

  • X0·e−θt — the fading memory. The starting value's influence shrinks toward zero as time passes. Bigger θ (stiffer spring) forgets the start faster.
  • m·(1 − e−θt) — the pull home. Starts at 0 and climbs to m. So early on the process still remembers X0; late on it has been dragged to m.
  • σ·∫e−θ(t−s)dW — the noise. An Itô integral, so its average is zero (a martingale, Lesson 013). It is what makes each path jiggle around the average instead of tracing it exactly.

The average. Because the noise term averages to zero, the mean of Xt is just the first two pieces:

E[Xt] = X0·e−θt + m·(1 − e−θt)

As t grows, e−θt → 0, so E[Xt] → m: on average it ends up exactly at the target. That is your predicted answer confirmed.

With real numbers. θ = 2, m = 100, X0 = 80. At t = 1: e−2 = 0.135, so E[X1] = 80·0.135 + 100·(1 − 0.135) = 10.8 + 86.5 = 97.3. Already almost home. The gap to m shrinks by half every half-life = ln 2 / θ = 0.693 / 2 = 0.35 units of time. (Read "half-life" like radioactive decay: the time for the remaining gap to m to halve.)

The spread. How far do paths jiggle from that average? The variance comes from the noise term. We need one tool from Lesson 013, re-warmed: the variance of an Itô integral ∫ g(s) dWs is ∫ g(s)² ds — the "Itô isometry." The plain reason: the shocks on different steps are independent (Lesson 012), and variances of independent things add; each little step contributes g(s)²·(variance of dW) = g(s)²·ds. Applying it with g(s) = σe−θ(t−s):

Var(Xt) = σ²·∫0t e−2θ(t−s) ds = (σ² / 2θ)·(1 − e−2θt)  ⟶  σ² / 2θ  (as t grows)

So after a while OU settles into a steady bell-curve wobble: normal, centred at m, with variance σ²/2θ. This never-changing long-run distribution is called the stationary distribution ("stationary" = it stops changing with time). Notice the tug-of-war in σ²/2θ: more shoving (σ) widens the wobble; a stiffer spring (θ) narrows it. That is the dashed band in the widget above.

With real numbers. σ = 10, θ = 2: long-run variance σ²/2θ = 100/4 = 25, so the long-run standard deviation is √25 = 5. The process ends up bouncing around 100 ± 5. Double the pull to θ = 4 and the spread shrinks to √(100/8) = 3.5: a stiffer spring holds it closer to home.

Why mean reversion is a big deal for your mission

OU is not an academic toy — it is one of the most-used models on a systematic desk, and it maps straight onto the alpha you are training to find.

  • Pairs trading / statistical arbitrage. Take two similar stocks (say two oil majors). Their price gap — the "spread" — tends to wander away and then snap back. Model that spread as OU: when it is far below m, the model says "it is pulled up" — so you buy the cheap one and short the rich one and wait for reversion. θ tells you how fast to expect the payoff; σ²/2θ tells you how wide the swings (and losses) can get before it reverts.
  • Interest-rate models. The Vasicek model of the short rate is literally an OU process: rates get pulled toward a long-run level. You will meet it again in the rates track.
  • Anything that "normalises." Volatility, inventory, order-book imbalance — many quantities revert. OU is the first, simplest tool for all of them, and the half-life ln2/θ is the number you quote to say "how long until it comes back."

When does an SDE even have a solution? (existence & uniqueness, in words)

We wrote down SDEs and solved two of them. But should we take for granted that an SDE has a solution at all — and only one? Not always. This is the "existence and uniqueness" question, and you only need the intuition, not the proof (the theorem is in Øksendal, today's reading). Two conditions on the drift a and diffusion b guarantee a single, sensible solution for all time. Both have plain-English meanings.

Condition 1 — bounded steepness ("Lipschitz"). The coefficients must not change infinitely fast as X changes: there is some finite ceiling K on how steeply a and b can respond to a change in X (|a(x) − a(y)| ≤ K|x − y|, and likewise for b). Why it matters: if two paths start a hair apart, bounded steepness keeps them from instantly flying apart — so the path is pinned down uniquely by its noise.
Condition 2 — no faster-than-linear growth. The coefficients must not grow faster than a straight line in X: |a(x)| ≤ C(1 + |x|), and likewise for b. Why it matters: if the drift rockets up faster than X itself, the process can accelerate so hard it reaches infinity in a finite time — it "blows up," and no solution exists past that instant.

See condition 2 fail. Forget randomness for a second (the drift alone causes the trouble). Compare two non-random rules from the same start x0: a linear-growth drift dx/dt = x (solution x0et — big, but finite forever) versus a super-linear drift dx/dt = x². The second solves to x(t) = x0/(1 − x0t), which shoots to infinity at t* = 1/x0 — a wall in finite time.

Why the drift can't grow too fast. Same start x0, two drifts. Linear growth dx/dt = x stays finite. Super-linear dx/dt = x² hits infinity at the dashed wall t* = 1/x0. Slide x0 up and the wall marches left — bigger starts blow up sooner. Faster-than-linear growth ⇒ no global solution; that is what condition 2 forbids.

The reassuring part. Both SDEs in this lesson pass both tests. GBM's coefficients μS and σS are straight lines in S (steepness capped at μ and σ; growth exactly linear). OU's θ(m − X) and σ are likewise linear (in fact OU's pull gets stronger as it strays, which only helps). So both are globally well-posed — one solution, for all time. The conditions are not red tape; they are the fine print that tells you your model will not silently explode.

With real numbers. dx/dt = x² from x0 = 2 blows up at t* = 1/2 = 0.5. From x0 = 0.5 it lasts until t* = 1/0.5 = 2. Meanwhile dx/dt = x from x0 = 2 reaches only 2e2 ≈ 14.8 at t = 2 — large, but perfectly finite. Same start, wildly different fate, purely because one drift grows too fast.

Explain it back

What this buys you

  • The simulation engine for everything ahead. Every pricing lab (Monte-Carlo options, Lesson 020) simulates GBM with the solved form — the −½σ² included. Every mean-reversion strategy simulates OU. You now know where both formulas come from.
  • The bridge to pricing. Lesson 015 (Girsanov) changes the drift μ to the risk-free rate r to get "risk-neutral" prices; Lesson 016 turns the GBM SDE into the Black–Scholes PDE by hedging away the dW. Both start from the SDE on this page.
  • Direct alpha. Fitting an OU process to a spread — estimating θ, m, σ — is the core of a pairs-trading signal. That is exactly the lab.
The honest warning (failure mode first, as always). A solved SDE is exact for its model, and both models here lie in the familiar ways. GBM inherits all of Brownian motion's flaws from Lesson 004: constant volatility (real vol clusters), Gaussian shocks (real tails are fat), no jumps (real prices gap). OU assumes the pull strength θ and level m are constant and known — but in a real spread they drift, and a "mean-reverting" pair can quietly stop reverting right when you have bet on it (the relationship breaks). Estimating θ from a short, noisy sample is famously biased toward "reverts faster than it really does." Treat the math as exact machinery; never confuse "exact given the model" with "true."
Trap — the six ways this material bites. (1) Forgetting the −½σ² when simulating GBM. Using S0eμt+σW (no correction) makes prices drift too high; the solved SDE carries μ − ½σ². (2) Reading θ(m − X) backwards. The pull is toward m: when X is below m the drift is positive (up). Flip the sign and you have an anti-reverting, exploding process. (3) Confusing the two spreads of OU. The mean marches to m; the wobble settles to σ²/2θ. They are different questions — where it centres vs how wide it bounces. (4) Expecting the noise term to add drift. σ∫e−θ(t−s)dW is an Itô integral: mean zero. All the average behaviour is in the first two pieces. (5) Adding an Itô correction to the OU solve. eθtX is linear in X (fxx=0), so there is no ½b²fxx term — unlike the GBM/log solve. Match the correction to the curvature. (6) Assuming every SDE has a nice solution. Most do not; a faster-than-linear drift can blow up in finite time. GBM and OU are the lucky, solvable, globally-well-posed cases.

Check your understanding

Common questions (and honest answers)

“Why can we solve GBM and OU by hand but almost nothing else?”
Because for these two there is a known function that cancels the X on the right — log S for GBM, eθtX for OU — turning the self-referential SDE into a plain integral. Most SDEs have no such lucky function, so the right side keeps depending on X and you cannot integrate in closed form. For those you simulate (step the recipe forward) or solve numerically. The two we can do by hand are exactly the two that carry most of the finance you will use, which is why they are worth memorising.
“The OU noise term still has X-free integrand e−θ(t−s) — isn't that cheating the ‘no X on the right' rule?”
No — the rule is that the right side must be free of X, and it is: the integrand e−θ(t−s) depends only on time, not on the current X. That is precisely why we can leave it as a (well-defined) Itô integral and still read off the mean (zero) and the variance (Itô isometry). If the integrand had contained X, we would be stuck.
“What exactly is an ‘integrating factor,' and how would I ever guess eθt?”
It is the same device from ordinary (non-random) differential equations: to solve dx/dt + θx = θm you multiply through by eθt so the left side becomes the neat derivative of eθtx. You "guess" it because the drift has a −θX term, and eθt is exactly what turns −θX into a cancellation. The SDE version is identical, with the bonus that eθtX is linear in X so Itô adds no correction. If you know the ODE trick, you know the SDE trick.
“Do quants really fit OU processes, or is this exam material?”
Really. Estimating (θ, m, σ) for a spread is a standard first pass at a pairs-trade signal, and the half-life ln2/θ is a number PMs ask for directly ("how long is this trade?"). The catch — a genuine interview and desk topic — is that θ estimated from short, noisy data is biased high (it looks like it reverts faster than it does), and a pair can stop reverting entirely. Knowing the model and its failure modes is the whole point.
“Why does existence/uniqueness get only intuition here, not a proof?”
Because the proof (a fixed-point / Picard-iteration argument, like the ODE version) is a page of analysis that does not change how you use SDEs, whereas the intuition — bounded steepness ⇒ one path; linear-growth ⇒ no blow-up — is what actually protects you from writing a model that silently explodes. The full statement and proof are in Øksendal Ch. 5 (today's reading) if you want the rigour; the two conditions and the x² blow-up picture are what to carry around.

Vocabulary you now own

  • Stochastic differential equation (SDE) — dX = a·dt + b·dW: a rule for how a random quantity nudges forward each step; drift a + random shove b·dW.
  • Drift / diffusion — the steady-pull coefficient a and the random-shove size b.
  • Solving an SDE — finding a function of X whose Itô expansion is free of X on the right, then integrating; gives a closed form for Xt.
  • Geometric Brownian motion (GBM) — dS = μS dt + σS dW, solved to St = S0e(μ−½σ²)t + σWt via Itô on log S.
  • Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) / mean reversion — dX = θ(m − X)dt + σ dW: a spring pulling X toward level m with strength θ.
  • Integrating factor — the multiplier eθt that cancels the −θX term and lets you integrate the OU SDE.
  • Long-run mean / stationary distribution — OU settles to N(m, σ²/2θ): centred at m, variance σ²/2θ.
  • Half-life of reversion — ln2/θ: the time for the gap to m to halve.
  • Itô isometry — the variance of ∫ g dW is ∫ g² ds (independent shocks, variances add).
  • Existence & uniqueness — bounded steepness (Lipschitz) ⇒ one path; no faster-than-linear growth ⇒ no finite-time blow-up.
