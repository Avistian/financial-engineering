The Black–Scholes PDE & Feynman–Kac

Lesson 016 · Why a differential equation and an average are the same price · ~90 min

Warm up

Read this first — how this lesson is paced. Same scaffolding as Lessons 014 and 015: every load-bearing equation gets an In plain words note that restates it as one sentence, and a with-real-numbers box that plugs in actual numbers. Nothing here needs a markets job or a finance degree. If a word has a special money-sense (book, hedge, short, option), it is defined in the same breath it appears. We re-state the pieces of Lessons 013–015 before we use them, so a rusty memory is not a blocker.

Here is the honest map for the hour — the same shape as Lessons 013–015, just a new object. First we re-state, from scratch, the ticket we are still pricing and the 10.45 Lesson 015 already found. Then we ask for a second description of that same number: a local balance rule, a partial differential equation. To get there we (1) picture the price as a hill over time and share-price, (2) re-warm Itô so we know how the hill moves, (3) cancel the random shove by selling the matching number of shares, and (4) demand that the leftover sure trickle earn only the bank rate. Out falls the Black–Scholes PDE. We then prove — slowly, every cancellation written out — that this local rule plus the ticket's final payout is yesterday's average. That dictionary is Feynman–Kac. We finish by seeing why the closed form you already own must solve the PDE, why a heat-equation change of variables is the reason N(d1) appears, and what the PDE cannot do.

The ticket we are still pricing

Keep the same concrete object as Lesson 015, restated from scratch because the words are easy to lose. A company share trades at S = 100 dollars today. You are looking at a ticket that says: on a fixed future date one year from now, you may buy one share for a fixed price of 100 — and you may tear the ticket up if you do not want to. If the share is then at 130, you use the ticket, pay 100, and pocket 30. If the share is at 80, you walk away and the ticket is worth nothing. So on the final date the ticket pays

(ST − 100)+  =  whichever is larger: ST − 100, or 0
In plain words Final payout = leftover of (share price minus 100), but never a negative number. You cannot be forced to buy a cheap share at an expensive fixed price.

People who trade these tickets call this a European call option. Each word is ordinary English used in a special way, so here they are, one at a time:

  • Option — everyday: a choice. Here: a contract that gives you a choice (use the ticket, or tear it up). It is not "a stock" and not "a preference."
  • Call — everyday: a phone call. Here: a ticket that lets you buy. The opposite ticket, a put, lets you sell. We will stay with the call.
  • European — everyday: from Europe. Here: you may use the ticket only on the final date, not earlier. (An "American" ticket can be used any day up to the final date. We are not pricing those today.)
  • Strike K — the fixed buy-price written on the ticket. Ours is K = 100, the same as today's share price. Traders say at the money for that coincidence; it just means "the ticket starts neither already winning nor already losing."

Two background numbers sit next to the share. Cash in the bank grows at a sure rate r = 5% per year (continuously compounded — a dollar today becomes e0.05 ≈ 1.051 in a year). The share wobbles with volatility σ = 20% per year: the size of the random shove, from Lesson 014. The share's real-world expected growth, μ = 15%, we will meet and then watch disappear.

Lesson 015 already priced this ticket at 10.4506 dollars. That number is our north star. Everything today must land on it.

Yesterday's answer, said slowly

Lesson 015's punchline: the 10.45 is not "what you expect the ticket to pay, shrunk back to today." That guess uses your personal forecast of the share, and different people have different forecasts, yet the ticket has one price. The price is the cost of a recipe that manufactures the ticket's payout out of shares and cash. That recipe is called a hedge — everyday: a garden hedge, a barrier. Here: an offsetting position that cancels the ticket's risk, the way a hedge blocks the wind. Once the risk is gone, no-arbitrage (no free money) pins the cost.

The surprise of 015 is that this cost can be rewritten as an average after all — provided you use a special set of weights, not your forecast. Those weights are the risk-neutral ones, written Q. Under them the share is pretended to grow only at the cash rate r, and the ticket's price is

Vt = EQ[ e−r(T−t) VT | ℱt ]
In plain words Today's price Vt = average, under the special weights Q, of (the ticket's final payout, shrunk back to today at the bank rate r), given what we know at time t. The shrink-factor e−r(T−t) is "a dollar on date T is worth less today, because cash in the bank would have grown." The real-world growth μ is not in the formula.
With real numbers. For our ticket, t = 0, T = 1, r = 0.05, so the shrink-factor is e−0.05 ≈ 0.951. One possible ending: the share finishes at 130, the ticket pays 30, and that 30 is worth 0.951 × 30 ≈ 28.5 today. Another ending: the share finishes at 80, the ticket pays 0, worth 0 today. Average those (and every other ending) with the Q-weights — not with "I think the share will rise" weights — and you get 10.45.

That is one description of the price: a global one. It looks at every possible future, averages, and shrinks. Today we derive the same 10.45 a second, independent way.

Why a second picture: weather map vs weather equation

Picture two ways to know tomorrow's temperature in a room. Way A: you have a giant list of possible weather histories, you average them, and you read off a number. Way B: you have a local rule that says "at this spot in the room, right now, the way temperature slants in space and the way it changes in time must balance, because heat cannot appear from nowhere." Both ways, done honestly, give the same temperature. They are not two guesses. They are one fact, written twice.

The list-of-histories is Lesson 015's average. The local balance rule is today's object: a partial differential equation, or PDE. "Differential equation" means a rule about how something changes. "Partial" means the something depends on more than one input — here, on time and on the share price — so the rule talks about several different slants at once. Contrast that with an ordinary (one-input) equation you already know: cash in the bank, dx = r x\,dt, depends only on time.

Why bother, if we already have the average? Three reasons, and they are the hour's map.

  • You will see the hedge work. The average hides the recipe. The PDE is the recipe written as "cancel the random shove, then the leftover must earn the bank rate." That is Lesson 015's "a price is a hedging cost," now in motion.
  • The two pictures are the same object. A theorem named Feynman–Kac (Richard Feynman and Mark Kac) is the dictionary: this local rule plus the ticket's final payout is yesterday's average. Not approximately. Exactly.
  • Most tickets have no formula. Barriers, averages-of-the-path, anything messy. Then you either simulate the average or solve the local rule on a grid. The Black–Scholes formula that gave us 10.45 is the rare case where the grid has an exact answer.

Predict before you read on

Before the algebra, commit to a picture. You own the ticket. Against it you sell some shares you do not own — you borrow them, sell them, and promise to give them back later. That is all shorting means here: sell now, buy back later. (Everyday "short" = not enough. Finance "short" = you have sold something borrowed.) The number of borrowed shares is written Δ (just a count; it can be a fraction). The recipe we will derive asks for about 0.64 shares. Suppose instead you borrow and sell only 0.30.

The price as a hill

The ticket's value depends on two things you can see right now: what time it is, and where the share is. Write that as a function of two inputs: V(t, s) — "if the clock says t and the share is at s dollars, the ticket is worth this many dollars." Picture a hill sitting over the (time, share price) plane. Height = ticket value. As the clock ticks and the share wobbles, you slide around on that hill. Today's job is to find the shape of the hill.

With real numbers — three points on the hill. Same ticket, three different "now"s, all computed from the formula you already own:

On the final date the hill is the kinked payout (0, 0, 20). A year earlier it is a smooth bump sitting above that kink. The extra height is "time value": the chance the kink still goes your way. We are looking for a rule that forces the hill to have exactly this shape, and no other.

Re-warm: how a hill changes (ordinary calculus, then Itô)

If the share moved smoothly — no random kick — you would already know how the height changes. Freeze the share and advance the clock a tiny bit dt: the height changes by Vt · dt, where Vt is the time slope ("how fast the hill drops if only the clock moves"). Freeze the clock and nudge the share by ds: the height changes by Vs · ds, where Vs is the space slope ("how fast the hill rises if only the share moves"). Add them: dV = Vt dt + Vs ds. That is ordinary calculus for a function of two inputs, and it is wrong for our share, because the share is jiggled by Brownian motion.

Lesson 013 told you what to add. When a quantity X moves by dX = a\,dt + b\,dW — a steady shove of size a per unit time, plus a random shove of size b — a smooth function f(t, x) of time and of X changes by

df = (ft + a fx + ½ b² fxx) dt + b fx dW
In plain words How f changes = (how the surface itself ages + slope times the steady shove + half the curvature times the shove-size squared) times the time step, plus (slope times the shove size) times the random kick. The extra half-curvature term is the Itô correction from Lesson 013: ordinary calculus would have dropped it.

Three marks on that formula. Everyday names first; the trader nicknames come after, and you do not need them to follow the argument.

  • ft is the time slope — how the hill's height changes if you freeze the share and only advance the clock. For our ticket this is usually negative: less time left means less chance the kink goes your way, so the ticket is worth less. Traders nickname Vt theta. We will just say time slope.
  • fx (for us, Vs) is the space slope — how the height changes if you freeze the clock and nudge the share by one dollar. If the share goes up one dollar and the ticket goes up 64 cents, the space slope is 0.64. That 0.64 is also "how many shares move like one ticket," which is why it will be the number we borrow and sell. Traders nickname it delta.
  • fxx (for us, Vss) is the curvature — how the space slope itself changes when the share moves. For the call it is positive: the kinked payout bends up, like a smile. A tiny extra bit of share-move is worth more when you are already a little in the winning region. Traders nickname it gamma.

Now put the share underneath the hill. Lesson 015 already moved us to the pricing world Q — same possible endings, different weights — where the share is treated as if it grew only at the bank rate: dS = r S\,dt + σ S\,dW̃. Read that as: over a tiny step, the share gets a sure shove of size r S (5% of 100 is 5 dollars per year) and a random shove of size σ S (20% of 100 is 20 dollars per square-root-year). So the two Itô ingredients are a = rS and b = σS. Plug those in with f = V(t, S):

dV = (Vt + r S Vs + ½ σ² S² Vss) dt + σ S Vs dW̃
In plain words The option's value changes by a predictable piece (time decay, plus the cash-rate carry on the slope, plus the Itô correction from curvature) and a random piece (slope times the stock's own random shove). The random piece is exactly Vs copies of the stock's dW̃.
With real numbers. Same market as Lesson 015: a one-year at-the-money call, S = 100, K = 100, r = 5%, σ = 20%, worth V = 10.4506 today. The three slopes, computed from the formula you already own, are:
SlopeNameValue at (t=0, S=100)
Vsdelta (space slope)0.6368

So a = rS = 5, b = σS = 20, and the predictable piece of dV is −6.414 + 5 × 0.6368 + ½ × 400 × 0.01876 = −6.414 + 3.184 + 3.752 = +0.523 dollars per year. Hold that 0.523 — it is not a coincidence. It equals r V = 0.05 × 10.4506. The PDE is the statement that this matching is forced.

Takeaway: Itô on V(t, S) splits the option's move into a predictable leftover and a random piece that is exactly Vs copies of the stock's random piece. That copy-count is what lets us cancel the noise.

Kill the noise: hold the ticket, sell the matching shares

Here is Lesson 015's recipe, now in continuous time, said without assuming you remember the two-state algebra. You hold one ticket. The ticket's random shove is σ S Vs dW̃ — "space slope times the share's own random shove." The share's random shove is σ S dW̃. So if you hold Vs shares, those two random pieces are the same size. If you sell that many shares (the short), the two random pieces point opposite ways and cancel.

Everyday picture: you own a boat that rocks with the waves. You also rent a second boat of a well-chosen size and point it the other way. The waves lift one and drop the other; the pair sits still. The well-chosen size is the space slope.

The pair — one ticket plus the sold shares — is a tiny book. Everyday: a notebook. Here: the bundle of positions you hold right now. Its value is

Π = V − Δ · S
In plain words Book value = option value minus (shares you are short) times (share price). If Δ = 0.64 and S = 100, you are short 64 dollars of stock against an option worth 10.45, so the book is worth 10.45 − 64 = −53.55 (you have borrowed, in effect).

Over a tiny step the bundle changes by dΠ = dV − Δ\,dS: the ticket moves, and you subtract the move of the shares you sold. We treat Δ as frozen for the step and then adjust it (rebalance). That "adjust as you go, without injecting extra cash" is what textbooks call self-financing — you are not topping up the recipe from your pocket at each step; sales of one piece pay for the other. Lesson 017 will measure what happens when you rebalance only once a day instead of every instant. Now substitute, the same slow-lane style as Lesson 014's OU cancel: write every term, then watch two pairs vanish.

Step 1 — write both moves, in full. From Itô: dV = (Vt + r S Vs + ½ σ² S² Vss) dt + σ S Vs dW̃. From the stock: dS = r S\,dt + σ S\,dW̃.
Step 2 — write Δ copies of the stock, in full. Δ\,dS = Δ·r S\,dt + Δ·σ S\,dW̃. Two piles: a sure piece of size Δ·r S per year, and a random piece of size Δ·σ S.
Step 3 — subtract, still with Δ unnamed. dΠ = dV − Δ\,dS is, collecting like with like, [Vt + r S Vs + ½ σ² S² Vss − Δ·r S] dt + [σ S Vs − Δ·σ S] dW̃. Legal because subtraction just lines the two dt piles up and the two dW̃ piles up. No assumption used.
Step 4 — choose Δ to kill the random pile. The coefficient of dW̃ is σ S (Vs − Δ). Set Δ = Vs, the space slope. Then Vs − Δ = 0, so the whole dW̃ pile is 0. The random kick is gone.
Step 5 — watch the matching sure terms cancel too. With that same Δ = Vs, the dt pile is Vt + r S Vs + ½ σ² S² Vss − Vs·r S. The middle two pieces are + r S Vs and − r S Vs — they cancel exactly, the same way +θ eθtX cancelled −θ eθtX in Lesson 014. What remains is only
dΠ = (Vt + ½ σ² S² Vss) dt
In plain words After you short exactly Vs shares, the book's next move has no random kick. It is a sure, tiny drift: time decay plus the Itô leftover from curvature. A book with no random kick is a riskless book.
With real numbers. At S = 100, Vs = 0.6368 and Vss = 0.01876, so the leftover drift is Vt + ½ σ² S² Vss = −6.414 + 3.752 = −2.662 dollars per year. Per trading day (dt ≈ 1/252) that is about minus one cent. The random piece, had you forgotten to hedge, would have been σ S Vs = 20 × 0.6368 ≈ 12.7 dollars per √year — about eighty cents a day. Hedging swapped an 80-cent coin-flip for a 1-cent sure trickle. That is the whole point of the recipe.

Slide the hedge ratio in the picture below. The paths are the leftover P&L of "long the call, short Δ shares, cash-financed," held for one month without rebalancing. Only at the Black–Scholes delta do they collapse to a thin band — and even there a little width remains, because a frozen Δ cannot track a moving slope. That leftover width is gamma, and it is the subject of Lesson 017.

Slider = shares shorted per call. The band is thinnest at Δ = N(d1) ≈ 0.637, which is Vs. Off that value, leftover stock noise fans the P&L out.

Takeaway: shorting Vs shares cancels the option's random shove against the stock's. What remains is a riskless trickle.

No free lunch: a sure leftover must earn the bank rate

After the random kick is cancelled, the bundle's next move is a sure trickle. A sure trickle is the same kind of thing as cash in the bank. Cash in the bank grows at r. So the trickle must grow at r too.

Why "must"? Because otherwise there is free money — what Lesson 015 called arbitrage: a trade that costs nothing today, can never lose, and might win. If the riskless bundle earned more than the bank, you would borrow from the bank (pay r) and hold the bundle (earn more than r); the difference is profit with no chance of loss. If the bundle earned less than the bank, you would do the opposite: sell the bundle, put the proceeds in the bank. In a working market those two free lunches cannot sit there, so the only leftover growth that is allowed is exactly r.

With real numbers. Our bundle is worth Π = 10.45 − 0.6368 × 100 = −53.23. The minus sign means you have, in effect, borrowed: you sold 63.68 dollars of share and only spent 10.45 on the ticket, so 53.23 dollars of cash landed in your pocket and must be accounted for. A borrowed 53.23, if it were a bank loan, would cost you 0.05 × 53.23 ≈ 2.66 dollars per year. That 2.66 will reappear in a moment as the leftover drift Vt + ½ σ² S² Vss = −6.414 + 3.752 = −2.662. The two numbers matching is the PDE, seen from the cash side.

In symbols: a riskless bundle of value Π must satisfy dΠ = r Π\,dt. That is the whole of "no free lunch" in one line. Substitute what we have:

Step 6 — write both sides. Left side, from the hedge: dΠ = (Vt + ½ σ² S² Vss) dt. Right side, from no-arbitrage: r Π\,dt = r(V − Vs S)\,dt. Why the right side looks like that: Π = V − Vs S is the book's value, and a riskless book of that value, parked at the bank, would earn r times itself per year.
Step 7 — cancel dt (legal: it is a common factor, not zero) and expand. Vt + ½ σ² S² Vss = r(V − Vs S) = r V − r S Vs. The last equality is just the distributive law.
Step 8 — move every term to the left. Add r S Vs and subtract r V on both sides (same operation on both sides, so the equality stays true):
Vt + r S Vs + ½ σ² S² Vss − r V = 0
In plain words Time decay, plus cash-rate carry on the slope, plus the curvature correction, minus the cash-rate funding of the option itself, must add to zero. That balance is the Black–Scholes PDE. It has to hold at every time and every stock price, or there is free money.
With real numbers — the four-term budget. At our (t, S) = (0, 100) point the four pieces are:
TermWhat it isDollars / year

Add them: −6.414 + 3.184 + 3.752 − 0.523 = 0.000. Not approximately — exactly, to machine precision, because the formula you already own is a solution of this equation. Drop any one term and the books do not balance. Drop the curvature term and you are 3.75 dollars/year short — ordinary calculus, and a wrong price.

Two remarks before we move. First, the −½σ² you have been carrying since Lesson 013 is in here, just wearing a different coat. Itô always adds "half the curvature times the shove-size squared." For log S the curve bends down (logs flatten as the share grows), so the leftover is a drag: −½σ². For the ticket the curve bends up (the kinked payout is U-shaped on the winning side), so the leftover ½ σ² S² Vss is a lift. Same rule. Opposite bend. Opposite sign. That is the sign-of-curvature habit this quarter has been building.

Second, there is an equivalent derivation that never mentions a bundle. Lesson 015 said a fairly priced ticket, after you shrink it back to today, is a martingale under Q — a fair game, no built-in drift. In symbols the shrunk ticket e−rt V(t, St) has no dt term. We can check that slowly.

Martingale route, step A. Let Ṽ = e−rt V. This is "the ticket's value, quoted in today's dollars." The shrink-factor e−rt is a smooth function of time only. Its own move is d(e−rt) = −r e−rt dt — ordinary calculus, no random kick.
Martingale route, step B. The product rule, plus Itô: because e−rt has no dW, there is no extra curvature leftover on the product (Lesson 013: a straight line in V has zero curvature). You get dṼ = −r e−rt V dt + e−rt dV.
Martingale route, step C. Substitute the dV we already have. The dt pile of dṼ is e−rt(Vt + r S Vs + ½ σ² S² Vss − r V). A fair game has no dt pile, so the thing in parentheses is zero. That is the same PDE.
In plain words "Hedge, then demand the leftover earn the bank rate" and "the shrunk ticket is a fair game" are two sentences for one equation. Use whichever picture sticks.

Takeaway: a cancelled random piece plus no free lunch is a differential equation. That equation is the Black–Scholes PDE.

Why μ never appears (Lesson 015, continuous time)

Look back at the PDE. The cash rate r is everywhere. The real-world drift μ is nowhere. This is the same shock as Lesson 015, where the real-world probability p never entered the two replication equations. Here is why, if you start the derivation in the real world instead of under Q.

Under P the stock is dS = μ S\,dt + σ S\,dW. Same slow lane as Steps 1–5, only the letter on the sure shove changes.

P-world, write both moves. Itô on the option now carries the real-world drift: dV = (Vt + μ S Vs + ½ σ² S² Vss) dt + σ S Vs dW. The stock is dS = μ S\,dt + σ S\,dW.
P-world, subtract Δ copies. dΠ = [Vt + μ S Vs + ½ σ² S² Vss − Δ·μ S] dt + [σ S Vs − Δ·σ S] dW.
P-world, the same Δ kills both extras. Set Δ = Vs. The random pile dies as before. The sure pile loses + μ S Vs against − Vs·μ S — they cancel exactly. What is left is dΠ = (Vt + ½ σ² S² Vss) dt — no μ. No-arbitrage still says this equals r Π\,dt, because a riskless leftover can earn only the bank rate, not the stock's hoped-for extra. Same PDE.
In plain words Whatever extra return the stock is expected to earn, the option is expected to earn the same extra on its stock-like part (the delta). Shorting that many shares subtracts the extra from both sides. After the hedge, nobody is being paid for risk, so only the cash rate can remain.
With real numbers. Suppose you wrongly wrote the PDE with μ = 15% in place of r = 5%. The leftover would be (μ − r) S Vs = 0.10 × 100 × 0.6368 = 6.37 dollars per year — the risk premium on the 0.64 shares, counted twice (once in the option, once not subtracted). That is not a small typo. On a 10.45 option it is a 60% relative error in the drift budget, and it is the continuous-time face of yesterday's "simulating with μ instead of r" bug.

Takeaway: μ cancels in the hedge, just as p cancelled in the two-state recipe. The PDE, like the price, cannot depend on anybody's forecast of drift.

The equation and its terminal condition

A differential equation by itself is a rule of motion; it does not pick out one function until you say where the motion ends. For an ordinary deposit, dx = r x\,dt needs a starting balance. For the option the natural extra fact is not a start but a finish: on date T the ticket is worth exactly its payoff, no arguments.

V(T, s) = (s − K)+
In plain words At the moment the ticket expires, its value is "whatever the share is worth above the strike, or zero." There is no time left to hedge, so there is no extra value. This is a terminal condition — a finish-line requirement — not a starting value.

Two more edges of the surface, so the rule is pinned down everywhere:

  • If the share is worthless, s = 0, it stays worthless (GBM at zero stays at zero). A call on a worthless share is worthless: V(t, 0) = 0.
  • If the share is extremely expensive, you will certainly use the ticket (traders say exercise — everyday: go to the gym; here: take the choice the ticket offers). Then the ticket is almost a promise to buy the share later at K. A promise to pay K on date T is worth only K e−r(T−t) today, because that is what you would need in the bank now to have K later. So V(t, s) ≈ s − K e−r(T−t), the share minus the already-shrunk strike. A contract that just swaps a share for cash later is called a forward; the expensive-share edge says "the ticket has become a forward."

Together, the PDE plus the finish line plus the two edges pick out exactly one surface V(t, s). That surface, evaluated at (0, 100), is 10.4506.

With real numbers. At expiry, τ = 0, the surface is the hockey-stick: V(T, 80) = 0, V(T, 100) = 0, V(T, 120) = 20. One year earlier the same three spots are worth 1.09, 10.45, and 24.51. The deep-in-the-money point rose only 4.51 above its payoff (time value on a ticket you will almost surely use); the at-the-money point rose from nothing to 10.45 (pure time value — the chance the kink goes your way).

Slide time-to-expiry in the picture. At τ = 0 you see the payoff, a kink at the strike. As you give the ticket more time, the kink is smoothed the way heat smooths a hot corner on a metal plate. That is not a metaphor we will make precise later — it is literally the same equation, after a change of variables.

Slider = years left until expiry. Dashed gold is the payoff max(S − K, 0). The green curve is today's price as a function of S. Time blurs the kink.

Takeaway: the PDE is the motion; the payoff at date T is the finish line. Together they pick out one price at every (t, s).

Feynman–Kac: two recipes, one cake

Everyday picture first, then the name. You have two recipes that claim to bake the same cake. Recipe A: list every possible future of the share, write down the ticket's payout on that future, shrink it back to today, and average with the special Q-weights. Recipe B: start from the kinked payout on the final date and walk backward, at every step enforcing "these slopes must add to zero." Feynman–Kac — named for the physicist Richard Feynman and the mathematician Mark Kac — is the theorem that the two recipes produce the same cake. Not two close cakes. One cake, two recipes.

You already watched this happen, without the name, in Lesson 015. The two-state tree said "today's price is the shrunk p*-average of the two children." That is Recipe A on a two-branch world. Rolling the tree back from the final date is Recipe B on the same world. They agreed because they had to. A 2,000-step tree and the formula agreed to a hundredth of a cent for the same reason.

You now have two descriptions of the same hill. Description A, from Lesson 015: at each (t, s), V is a shrunk Q-average of the finish-line payout. Description B, from this lesson: V solves a local balance rule with that payout as its finish line. Feynman–Kac says these are one answer, written twice.

The general statement — still in our notation, no extra machinery — is this. Suppose a quantity X moves by dX = a(t, X)\,dt + b(t, X)\,dW, and a function u(t, x) satisfies

ut + a ux + ½ b² uxx − r u = 0,    with    u(T, x) = g(x)
In plain words If a surface obeys "time slope + drift times space slope + half diffusion-squared times curvature − funding cost = 0," and equals a known payoff g at the finish, then the height at (t, x) equals the discounted average of g(XT) starting from Xt = x.

That "then" is a theorem, not a definition. Here is why it is true, every cancellation written out — the same slow-lane habit as Lesson 014's integrating factor and Lesson 015's completing-the-square. The idea: start at time t with Xt = x, ride along the surface u as X wobbles, shrink the height back to time-t dollars as you go, and watch the predictable leftover vanish because the PDE said it had to.

FK, step A — name the riding process. Fix a starting time t and a starting spot x. For later times s between t and T, write Ys = e−r(s−t) u(s, Xs). Read that as: "the surface's height at the random place X has reached by time s, already shrunk back to time-t money." At the start, s = t, the shrink-factor is e0 = 1 and Xt = x, so Yt = u(t, x) — the number we want. At the finish, s = T, the surface equals the payoff, so YT = e−r(T−t) g(XT) — the number we average.
FK, step B — Itô on the product. Y is a product of a sure shrink-factor e−r(s−t) (a function of time only) and the surface u(s, Xs). The shrink-factor moves by ordinary calculus: d(e−r(s−t)) = −r e−r(s−t) ds, no random kick. Itô on u is the formula we have been using all hour: du = (ut + a ux + ½ b² uxx) ds + b ux dW. The product rule, plus Itô: because the shrink-factor has no dW, there is no extra curvature leftover on the product (Lesson 013: a straight line in u has zero second derivative). So dY = −r e−r(s−t) u\,ds + e−r(s−t) du.
FK, step C — substitute du and collect the sure pile. The ds coefficient of dY is e−r(s−t)(ut + a ux + ½ b² uxx − r u). Look at the parenthesis: that is exactly the left-hand side of the PDE we assumed u solves. The PDE says it is 0. So the sure pile of dY is 0. What remains is only a random kick: dY = e−r(s−t) b ux dW.
FK, step D — a process with no sure pile is a fair game. Re-warm Lesson 013: any ∫ … dW is a martingale — a fair game whose best forecast of the next value is the value you have now. So Yt = E[YT | Xt = x]. Substitute the two ends from step A:
u(t, x) = E[ e−r(T−t) g(XT) | Xt = x ]
In plain words Ride the surface, shrink as you go, and the PDE cancels every predictable leftover. A leftover-free ride is a fair game, so today's height equals the average of the shrunk finish-line payout. That is the whole theorem: the local balance forces the global average.

Specialise to Black–Scholes: a = r s, b = σ s, g(s) = (s − K)+, and the expectation is taken under Q (because that is the world in which the share's drift is r). You recover yesterday's pricing formula on the left and today's PDE on the right. Either side can be taken as the definition of the price; the other is then a theorem.

One compact nickname, now that you have earned it. The combination a ∂x + ½ b² ∂xx — "drift times space slope, plus half diffusion-squared times curvature" — is called the infinitesimal generator of the process X, written 𝒜. Everyday picture: it is the local recipe that turns a function of the current state into the sure part of how that function is about to move. The PDE is then one short sentence: ut + 𝒜u − r u = 0. You do not need the name to use the PDE. You need it because every later process (jumps in 018, random volatility) is described by writing down its generator and dropping it into this same slot.

With real numbers. Description A, one path: draw Z ~ N(0,1), set ST = 100 · e(0.05 − ½·0.04)·1 + 0.20·Z = 100 · e0.03 + 0.20 Z, write down e−0.05 max(ST − 100, 0). Average 50,000 independent draws and you get about 10.45. Description B: solve the PDE on a grid (or, in this special case, read off the closed form) and you get 10.4506. The lab makes you run both machines on the same inputs and watch them agree. The picture below is the same check drawn as a curve: the green line is the PDE solution against today's stock price; the red dots are a Monte-Carlo average at seven spots. Slide the path-count up and the dots sit on the line.

Green: the function that solves the PDE. Red: discounted Monte-Carlo payoffs at seven starting prices. Slider = number of paths. Feynman–Kac is the landing.

Why this is more than a curiosity. Most tickets you will price have no closed form — barriers, Asians, anything with a messy payoff. Feynman–Kac says you still have two numerical machines, and they must agree:

  • The expectation machine (Lesson 019): simulate many paths under Q, average the discounted payoff. Easy to code; noisy; shines in high dimension.
  • The PDE machine: replace the derivatives by finite differences on a grid in (t, s) and step backward from the finish line. Quiet; awkward above two or three state variables.

A binomial tree is the PDE machine in disguise: each node is the local one-step version of the same balance, and rolling the tree back from expiry is solving the PDE. That is why Lesson 015's tree and Lesson 015's formula agreed. They were already Feynman–Kac, before we had the name.

Takeaway: Feynman–Kac is the dictionary. "Solve the PDE" and "take the discounted Q-average" are two spellings of one price.

The formula you already own solves this PDE

Re-state the formula from Lesson 015, every symbol defined, because we are about to lean on it. A European call — our ticket — is worth

C = S N(d1) − K e−rτ N(d2)
d1 = [ ln(S/K) + (r + ½σ²)τ ] / (σ√τ),    d2 = d1 − σ√τ
In plain words The ticket is worth "the share you might receive, minus the cash you might have to pay" — each counted only in the worlds where you actually use the ticket. τ = T − t is time left. N(·) is the standard-normal cumulative from Lesson 005: N(x) is the chance a bell-curve draw lands below x. N(d2) is the risk-neutral chance you use the ticket, so K e−rτ N(d2) is the already-shrunk strike you expect to pay. S N(d1) is the value of the share you expect to receive, weighted a little more heavily than a plain probability because the worlds where you use the ticket are also the worlds where the share is worth more.
With real numbers — the same 10.45, written out again. S = 100, K = 100, r = 0.05, σ = 0.20, τ = 1. Then ln(100/100) = 0, so d1 = (0 + (0.05 + 0.02)·1) / 0.20 = 0.07 / 0.20 = 0.35 and d2 = 0.35 − 0.20 = 0.15. From the normal table, N(0.35) = 0.6368 and N(0.15) = 0.5596, and e−0.05 = 0.9512. So C = 100 × 0.6368 − 100 × 0.9512 × 0.5596 = 63.68 − 53.23 = 10.45. That 0.6368 is also today's space slope Vs — the hedge ratio we have been using all hour. Not a coincidence: for a call, Vs = N(d1).

We have claimed, twice, that this formula satisfies the PDE. The lab will grind the derivatives and check the residual is machine-zero on a grid. Here is the honest shorter reason it must work, without grinding.

Lesson 015 derived that formula as one risk-neutral expectation. Feynman–Kac, the four-step argument we just wrote, says every such expectation solves the corresponding PDE. Therefore the formula solves the PDE. The chain has no gap: the only thing to check is that we specialise Feynman–Kac to the right a, b, r, and g — and we just did.

What you should be able to do cold is the four-term budget at one point, because that is how you catch a broken implementation. If a colleague's pricer returns 10.45 but their Greeks give Vt + r S Vs + ½ σ² S² Vss − r V = 3.75, they have dropped the curvature term. If the residual is 6.37, they put μ in the PDE. The residual is a unit test for the whole theory.

With real numbers — three broken residuals. The correct residual at (0, 100) is 0.000. The same Greeks, fed to a damaged equation:
DamageWhat you computedResidual
drop ½ σ² S² Vssordinary calculus−3.752
write μ instead of r on the slopethe forecast bug+6.368

Three different bugs, three different leftover numbers, each one equal to the term you dropped (or to (μ − r) S Vs). The residual tells you which piece is missing.

Worked example 2: a put, same PDE

Lesson 013 drove Itô home on two functions; Lesson 014 solved two SDEs. Here is today's second object, so the PDE does not stay glued to one ticket. A European put is the opposite ticket: it lets you sell one share at the fixed price K on the final date, and lets you tear it up if you do not want to. If the share finishes at 80 and the ticket lets you sell at 100, you use it and pocket 20. If the share finishes at 130, you walk away. Final payout: (K − ST)+.

The hedge argument never used the shape of the payout — only that the value is some smooth surface V(t, s) and that the share is dS = r S dt + σ S dW̃. So a put obeys the same PDE. What changes is the finish line: P(T, s) = (K − s)+. The cheap-share edge is now P(t, 0) ≈ K e−r(T−t) (a worthless share makes the put a sure cash K later), and the expensive-share edge is P(t, s) → 0 (you will not sell an expensive share at K).

With real numbers — put-call parity is the sanity check. Same market: S = K = 100, r = 5%, σ = 20%, one year. The call was 10.45. The put, from the same formula with the signs flipped (P = K e−rτ N(−d2) − S N(−d1)), is 5.57. Check: call − put = 10.45 − 5.57 = 4.88, and S − K e−rτ = 100 − 95.12 = 4.88. That identity — put-call parity — says "a call minus a put is a forward": buying the call and selling the put manufactures a promise to buy the share later at K, which is worth the share minus the already-shrunk strike. If a colleague's put pricer returns 7 against our 10.45 call, you do not need the PDE: parity is already broken.

And because the PDE is linear (every term has exactly one factor of V or a slope of V), the difference C − P itself solves the PDE, with finish line (s − K)+ − (K − s)+ = s − K. The unique solution of that problem is s − K e−r(T−t) — the forward. Parity is not an extra fact. It is the PDE, run on a simpler ticket.

Takeaway: the PDE comes from the share and the hedge, not from the payout. A put is the same motion rule with a different finish line. Put-call parity is that rule applied to C − P.

Why a closed form exists (the heat equation)

Most PDEs do not have a formula. This one does, and the reason is a change of variables that turns it into the oldest differential equation in physics: the heat equation, which says that temperature at a point changes in proportion to the curvature of the temperature around it — heat flows from hot to cold and flattens bumps.

You do not need to reproduce the substitutions in an interview. You do need the picture. Heat in a metal bar: a hot corner cools because heat flows from hot to cold, and the flow is faster where the temperature is more curved. The equation is "how temperature changes in time = a constant times how curved it is in space." Our hill, after three changes of label, is that equation.

The three label-changes, each with a job. We will do the first one in the slow lane so you see what "a change of variables" actually does to a PDE, then name the other two and read off the result.

Label-change 1 — flip the clock. Let τ = T − t be time left. The finish line is then τ = 0, and walking forward in τ is walking backward on the calendar — the direction a PDE solver actually steps. The chain rule: Vt = Vτ · ∂τ/∂t = Vτ · (−1) = − Vτ. Legal because τ depends only on t, so the space slopes Vs and Vss are untouched. Substitute into the PDE: − Vτ + r S Vs + ½ σ² S² Vss − r V = 0, or Vτ = r S Vs + ½ σ² S² Vss − r V. In words: as expiry approaches, the hill changes by the cash-rate carry, plus the curvature leftover, minus the funding cost.
With real numbers — the flipped clock at our point. Today, τ = 1 and we already have Vt = −6.414. After the flip, Vτ = − Vt = +6.414: as you give the ticket more time (increase τ), the hill rises by 6.414 dollars per year. That matches the everyday fact that a longer ticket is worth more. Check the other side of the flipped PDE: r S Vs + ½ σ² S² Vss − r V = 3.184 + 3.752 − 0.523 = 6.413, which is − Vt again. Same four-term budget, just read "how the hill grows as you add time" instead of "how it drops as the clock advances."
Label-change 2 — take logs. Let x = log(s/K). The share's "multiply by a random factor" wobble becomes an "add a random amount" wobble — logs turn products into sums, which is why Lesson 014 solved GBM by applying Itô to log S. After this change, the ugly S and S² coefficients in front of the slopes become constants. You do not need the algebra; you need the reason: GBM is a multiplier, and a log turns a multiplier into a shifter.
Label-change 3 — peel off the funding. Write V = K e−α τ u(τ, x) and choose the constant α so the − r V funding term and a leftover first-slope term are absorbed into the exponential prefactor. What remains of u has no zeroth-order piece and no first-slope piece — only time-change and curvature.

After those three relabellings,

uτ = ½ σ² uxx
In plain words How the transformed price changes as expiry approaches = a constant times how curved it is in log-price. That is the heat equation. The constant ½ σ² is the thermal diffusivity — here, how fast uncertainty blurs the payoff kink.

The heat equation on the whole line, started from a hockey-stick, is solved by smearing the hockey-stick against a Gaussian kernel. That kernel is why N(d1) and N(d2) appear: they are the integrals of a normal density against the two halves of the payoff. You do not need to reproduce the substitutions in an interview; you do need to know that the normal CDF is in the formula because the PDE is heat, and heat's fundamental solution is a Gaussian.

Takeaway: Black–Scholes is the heat equation in market variables. The N(·) in the formula is the heat kernel, not a coincidence.

What the PDE cannot do

The derivation assumed a world that Lesson 015 already flagged as a fiction: one Brownian motion, constant r and σ, continuous frictionless rebalancing, a European finish line. Relax any of those and the PDE changes — or stops being enough.

  • Early exercise (American tickets). You may now choose to take the payoff before date T. The finish line is no longer the only constraint: the surface must also stay above the payoff at every earlier time, and the region where it is optimal to exercise becomes an unknown moving boundary. The PDE still holds where you have not exercised; finding where that is is a different problem. European Black–Scholes will misprice an American put, often by a lot.
  • Jumps or random volatility. Extra sources of risk that you cannot trade away. Lesson 015's FTAP 2: the market is incomplete, many Q's survive, and no-arbitrage no longer pins one PDE. Desks then choose a Q by fitting the options that already trade. Lesson 018 starts that story.
  • Discrete hedging. The derivation freezes Δ for an instant and then rebalances. In life you rebalance on a schedule. The cancelled dW is not quite cancelled; the leftover is a gamma P&L that is sometimes a gift and sometimes a bill. Lesson 017 measures it.
Trap — the six ways this material bites. (1) Putting μ in the PDE. The continuous-time face of yesterday's "simulate with μ" bug. The hedge cancels μ; the residual of the damaged equation is (μ − r) S Vs = 6.37 in our numbers. If your PDE mentions the real-world drift, it is not a pricing equation. (2) Dropping ½ σ² S² Vss. Ordinary calculus. You are silently assuming the option is a straight line in the stock. Residual −3.75 at our point — the same size as the Itô correction you have been carrying since Lesson 013, with the opposite sign because the call curves up. (3) Writing the payoff as a starting condition. The PDE runs backward from date T. Setting V(0, s) = (s − K)+ prices a ticket that expired today and has already paid out. (4) Forgetting − r V. The option itself is an asset that must be funded. Dropping the term is claiming a 10.45 ticket can sit on the books for free. Residual +0.52. (5) Treating the PDE as a forecast. Same P-versus- Q confusion as yesterday. The PDE uses r because it is a pricing identity, not because you believe the stock grows at r. (6) Feeding an American ticket to a European solver. Early exercise is a free boundary, not a terminal condition. The European number is a floor (for calls on non-dividend stocks it is also exact — a rare kindness), not a general recipe.

Explain it back

What this buys you

  • A second, independent price. At the Lesson 020 checkpoint you will price one European option three ways — closed form, Monte-Carlo, PDE — and reconcile them to tolerance. Today's derivation is the PDE route; yesterday's expectation is the Monte-Carlo route; Feynman–Kac is why they must match.
  • A unit test for every pricer you will write. Plug the output and its Greeks into Vt + r S Vs + ½ σ² S² Vss − r V. If that is not zero, the implementation is wrong, even if the price "looks about right."
  • The Greeks, for free. Delta, gamma, and theta are not extra decorations; they are the slopes the PDE is written in. Lesson 017 will trade them. You already know their values at one point, and you already know they balance.
  • A reason the normal CDF is in the formula. Heat kernel, not numerology. That is the interview-length answer to "why N(d1)?"
The honest warning (failure mode first, as always). The PDE is exact given the model, and the model is the same fiction as yesterday: continuous hedging, constant volatility, no jumps, no costs. Every desk that uses this equation uses it as a reference point, not as a photograph of the world. "The books balance in the Black–Scholes universe" is a true and useful sentence. "Therefore this is what the option is worth in the market" is a different sentence, and it is not free.

Check your understanding

Common questions (and honest answers)

“If I already have the expectation, why do I need a PDE at all?”
Because the two machines have opposite strengths. An average over paths is easy to write and scales to many risk sources; a grid is quiet and gives you every Greek as a by-product of the solve. Desks keep both. Feynman–Kac is the promise that they are not allowed to disagree — so when they do, you have a bug, not a "different model."
“Where did the market price of risk θ go? Yesterday it was the whole story.”
It did its job and left. θ = (μ − r)/σ is how you got to the pricing world Q, where the stock's drift is already r. Once you are under Q, the PDE is written entirely in r, σ, V and its slopes. If you start the hedge under P instead, μ cancels and θ never needs to be named. Either way it does not appear in the final equation — which is the point.
“Is the −½σ² from d(log S) the same object as ½σ²S² Vss?”
Same rule, different function. Itô always adds ½ b² fxx. For f = log S you have b = σS and fxx = −1/S², so the correction is −½ σ² and the log-price is dragged down. For f = V you have the same b and Vss > 0 (a call is convex), so the correction ½ σ² S² Vss is a lift. Sign of curvature decides sign of the leftover. That is the rule you were asked to make automatic before this lesson.
“Can I use this PDE for a put? For a digital? For a barrier?”
Same PDE, different finish line (and, for the barrier, an extra edge where the ticket knocks out and V = 0). The motion rule comes from the stock and the hedge; the payoff is what you attach at date T (and on any extra boundary the contract writes down). A put is V(T, s) = (K − s)+ and the same four-term budget. A digital that pays 1 if ST > K has a discontinuous finish line — still legal, just harder to compute, which is why people prefer the expectation machine for it.
“You said a binomial tree is a PDE solver. Is the CRR tree from yesterday literally this equation?”
Yes, in discrete form. At each node the tree says "today's value is the discounted p*-average of the two children." Expand the children in a Taylor series in the step Δt, collect terms, and the PDE falls out as Δt → 0. That is why a 2,000-step tree and the formula agreed to a hundredth of a cent in the Lesson 015 lab: the tree was solving today's equation without announcing it.
“What does ‘short’ actually mean? Do I need a broker who lends shares?”
For the argument, "short Δ shares" just means "your bundle's value moves opposite to those shares." In a real account, yes, you borrow the shares (or the futures market builds the same exposure without a stock-loan). The derivation does not depend on the plumbing. What it depends on is: you can hold a fractional, negative amount of the share and mark it to the current price.
“Why is the time slope negative? Doesn't a longer ticket have more ways to win?”
A longer ticket is worth more — that is why, at a fixed date, a one-year ticket beats a one-month ticket. The time slope Vt asks a different question: freeze the share and advance the clock toward the finish. You have less time left, so the ticket is worth less. Negative time slope is "the ticket ages," not "longer tickets are cheaper."
“Is 10.45 the money I should expect to make if I buy the ticket?”
No. 10.45 is what it costs to manufacture the payout, and therefore the fair sale price. What you expect to make as a buyer depends on the real-world growth μ and on how you hedge. If you buy at 10.45 and the share drifts up faster than the bank rate, you expect to profit; if it does not, you expect to lose. The price does not promise you a profit. It promises that nobody can lock in a risk-free profit against you.
“Feynman–Kac looked like a proof. Did we actually prove uniqueness too?”
Almost. The four-step argument showed: if a surface solves the PDE with the given finish line, then it equals the discounted expectation. The expectation is one number, so there cannot be two different solutions — uniqueness is free. Existence is the other direction: the discounted expectation, under mild conditions on g and on the coefficients, is smooth enough to be fed back into Itô and shown to solve the PDE. For our call the closed form is that existence proof, in a box. For a messy payoff you trust a theorem (Shreve II §6.4) rather than writing the formula.
“Why is put-call parity true even if I do not believe Black–Scholes?”
Because it does not use the PDE's coefficients — only no-arbitrage and the two finish lines. Buying a call and selling a put of the same strike and expiry manufactures a forward: you will buy the share at K on date T no matter where it finishes. A forward's price is S − K e−r(T−t) as long as you can hold the share and borrow cash, which is a much thinner model than GBM. That is why a broken parity is a bug, not a "different volatility."

Vocabulary you now own

  • Price surface V(t, s) — the option's value as a function of the clock and of the current share price; a hill you slide on as time passes and the share wobbles.
  • Delta Vs — the space slope of that hill; the number of shares that cancels the option's random shove. Equals N(d1) for a Black–Scholes call.
  • Gamma Vss — the curvature of the hill; how fast the hedge ratio itself moves when the share moves.
  • Theta Vt — the time slope; how the ticket ages if the share is frozen. Usually negative for a long call.
  • Delta-hedged book Π = V − Vs S — one option against the cancelling number of shares; instantaneously riskless.
  • Black–Scholes PDE — Vt + r S Vs + ½ σ² S² Vss − r V = 0; the local no-arbitrage balance after the noise is cancelled.
  • European put — the opposite ticket: the right to sell one share at K on date T; pays (K − ST)+. Same PDE, different finish line.
  • Put-call parity — C − P = S − K e−r(T−t); a call minus a put is a forward. True by no-arbitrage, and also the PDE run on C − P.
  • Terminal condition — the finish-line requirement V(T, s) = g(s); for a call, (s − K)+.
  • Feynman–Kac theorem — a PDE of this form plus a terminal condition is the same object as a discounted expectation of that payoff along the matching diffusion.
  • Infinitesimal generator — the local recipe a ∂s + ½ b² ∂ss that turns a function of the state into the drift of that function; the PDE says Vt + 𝒜V − rV = 0.
  • Heat equation — uτ = ½ σ² uxx, what the Black–Scholes PDE becomes after a change of variables; the reason the formula contains a normal CDF.
  • PDE residual — the left-hand side of the PDE, evaluated on a candidate surface; zero (to numerical noise) if and only if the surface is a solution. A unit test.
