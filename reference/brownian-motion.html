Martingale facts

StatementWhy
E[Wt | ℱs] = Wstake out the known Ws; the increment is independent of the past with mean 0
E[Wt² | ℱs] = Ws² + (t − s)expand (Ws+ΔW)²; the cross term vanishes, E[ΔW²]=Var=t−s
Wt² − t is a martingalethe leftover t is the quadratic variation (the compensator)

Quadratic variation — the headline

[W]t = limmesh→0 Σ (Wti+1 − Wti)² = t

Slow-lane reason: with m pieces of width Δt = t/m, the sum Q = Σ(ΔW)² has mean m·Δt = t (mean square move = variance = Δt) and variance m·2(Δt)² = 2t²/m → 0. Mean t, vanishing wobble ⇒ Q → t with certainty.

CurveΣ(Δ·)² (squared)Σ|Δ·| (first-order)
smooth f (finite slope)→ 0 (a straight line's is 1/m)finite (its length)
Brownian motion→ t (does not vanish)→ ∞ (path length is infinite)
The rule you compute with: (dW)² = dt. Keep any (dW)² term and replace it by dt; drop dW·dt and (dt)² (they shrink faster than dt). This single non-vanishing second-order term is the whole difference between ordinary and Itô calculus (Lesson 013).

Continuous but nowhere differentiable

slope over Δt  ≈  |ΔW| / Δt  ≈  √Δt / Δt  =  1/√Δt  →  ∞

Continuous because the move √Δt → 0; not differentiable because the slope 1/√Δt → ∞. This is why we write dW, never W′(t).

Simulate it (what the lab does)

Wt+Δt = Wt + √Δt · Z,    Z ~ N(0, 1) i.i.d.

Valid Brownian samples at the grid points. Realized variance — the summed squared returns — is the data version of [W]t, the workhorse volatility estimator (Year 1 Q3).

Traps. (1) Thinking continuous ⇒ smooth (it is nowhere differentiable). (2) Dropping (dW)² — it is first-order, ≈ dt. (3) Scaling space like t instead of √t (dividing by n not √n collapses the walk). (4) Confusing Var(Wt)=t (across many paths) with [W]t=t (one path's squared wiggles). (5) Treating Gaussian increments as realistic — they miss the fat tails and volatility clustering of Lesson 004. (6) Forgetting increments are independent of the past — that independence is what makes Wt a martingale.
